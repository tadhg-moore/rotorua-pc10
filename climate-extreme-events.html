Climate-Scenario Extreme & Storm-Event Analysis

climate-scenario-workflow.qmd builds scenario_hourly_met – the AEME MET_*-formatted hourly forcing for every GCM x SSP x future-window combination (7 GCMs x 4 SSPs x 3 windows, minus NZESM/ssp585 = 81 branches), by applying each GCM/scenario’s monthly delta-change signal to the bias-corrected daily ERA5 baseline and disaggregating back to hourly against the bias-corrected hourly ERA5 record.

This page uses that same hourly forcing to ask a narrower question: how does the frequency and intensity of extreme/storm-scale events change, not just the monthly-mean climate? Sub-daily (hourly) data matters here because heavy-rain intensity, wind-storm duration and short heatwaves are sub-daily-to-daily phenomena that a monthly delta-change factor alone cannot resolve – they only show up once the delta has been re-expanded to hourly.

This is a rebuild of an earlier version of this page, incorporating two changes made since:

  1. A rainfall unit bug fix. met-source-comparison.qmd caught MET_pprain reading ~1000x too high from ERA5’s raw units; every rainfall figure below is recomputed against the corrected series.
  2. Wind is now disaggregated as a plain scalar, not a vector. metscale::disaggregate_met_to_hourly() was reshaping wind via its u/v vector components, which was found to inflate disaggregated hourly wind speed beyond its own delta-adjusted daily target. apply_gcm_delta_to_baseline() and the scenario_hourly_met target now strip MET_wnduvu/MET_wnduvv/MET_wnddir before disaggregation, so wind speed is shaped directly from the donor’s own wind-speed pattern – direction isn’t needed for this lake model. See the wind section below for the corrected numbers.
  3. Rainfall thresholds now use the 125-year Whakarewarewa rain-gauge record (pc10_climate_met, via BOPRC/PC10 – see met-source-comparison.qmd) instead of the 20-year ERA5 window, and a return-level table is now possible for the first time.

Method

For every branch of scenario_hourly_met, and separately for the bias-corrected historical baseline (era5_corrected_hourly, 1995-2014), compute_annual_extreme_indices() (R/summarise_climate_extremes.R) computes one row per calendar year for:

Index Definition
rx1day Annual maximum daily rainfall (mm)
heavy_rain_days Days/year with daily rainfall above the p95 (wet days only)
extreme_rain_days Days/year above the p99
hot_days Days/year with daily maximum air temperature above the historical p95
wsdi Warm-spell duration index: days/year in spells of >=3 consecutive hot days
max_wind Annual maximum hourly wind speed (m/s)
storm_wind_hours Hours/year with hourly wind speed above the historical p95
severe_wind_hours Hours/year above the historical p99
storm_events Number of separate contiguous spells/year above the wind p95 threshold

Thresholds (compute_extreme_thresholds()) are fixed once and applied unchanged to every future branch, so “heavy rain” and “storm wind” mean the same absolute thing in every period – a change in frequency is then a genuine intensification/frequency signal, not an artefact of a moving definition. Wind and temperature thresholds still come from the bias-corrected ERA5 hourly baseline (1995-2014) – there’s no long-record alternative for those at this site. Rainfall thresholds now come from the 125-year Whakarewarewa daily rain-gauge record (compute_long_rain_thresholds(), on pc10_climate_met) rather than the 20-year ERA5 window: 20 years is thin for estimating a p95/p99 event, and the long, independently-measured record is unaffected by anything in the reanalysis/bias-correction/delta-change chain the scenarios themselves are built from.

Long-record return levels

With 125 years of daily rainfall, annual-maximum return levels can be estimated directly, well beyond what any 20-30 year GCM/reanalysis window here could support. compute_rain_return_levels() fits a Gumbel (extreme-value type I) distribution to the annual maxima by the method of moments:

These are a fixed, historically-grounded reference point – e.g. how the scenario ensemble’s projected rx1day (below) compares to a present-day 1-in-20 or 1-in-50 year event – independent of any GCM or bias-correction choice made elsewhere in this pipeline.

Discussion

Rainfall. heavy_rain_days and extreme_rain_days increase modestly and roughly monotonically with emissions scenario and window, consistent with a warmer atmosphere holding more moisture – but the ensemble range across GCMs is wide relative to the mean shift, and for rx1day several GCMs project little change or a slight decrease at some windows (the ratio-based delta-change method rescales the historical daily rainfall sequence rather than restructuring its day-to-day intermittency, so it cannot invent a heavier single-day extreme than proportional scaling of the historical record allows – see the stationarity caveat in climate-scenario-workflow.qmd). Anchoring the rainfall thresholds to the 125-year Whakarewarewa record instead of the 20-year ERA5 window matters here specifically because a 20-year sample gives a noisy p95/p99 estimate – with a long, independent record the “heavy”/“extreme” definitions themselves are far more stable, so a projected change in frequency is more trustworthy as an actual signal rather than partly an artefact of which 20 years happened to set the threshold.

Heat. hot_days and wsdi (warm-spell duration) show the clearest, most consistent scenario/window gradient of the three groups – both increase substantially and near-monotonically from ssp126 to ssp585 and from the near-term to the end-of-century window, with comparatively narrow GCM spread. This is the expected signature of a monotonic mean-warming delta applied to a fixed threshold: as the whole distribution shifts up, an increasing share of days cross a fixed, historical p95 threshold.

Wind storms. With the vector-disaggregation fix, wind speed is now shaped directly from the donor’s own hourly wind-speed pattern rather than being recomputed from reshaped u/v components, removing the disaggregation-side inflation flagged in the previous version of this page (disaggregated hourly wind no longer runs systematically higher than its own delta-adjusted daily target). The wind-storm indices above should now be read as a direct read on the GCM ensemble’s wind delta-change signal, which – per gcm_monthly_deltas_df – is close to 1 (little change) for most GCM/scenario combinations, with a modest number of outlier models/months driving any larger shifts. Wind remains the index group with the least physical basis for a strong climate-change signal at monthly delta-change resolution, since storm-scale wind events are a synoptic-scale phenomenon that a monthly mean factor is a weak proxy for.

Practical implication for lake modelling. Heavy-rain and warm-spell frequency both increase with scenario severity in a way that is directionally robust across the GCM ensemble, and both are levers on lake response that matter beyond the monthly-mean forcing already explored in climate-scenario-workflow.qmd: more frequent heavy-rain days translate to more frequent high-inflow/nutrient-loading pulses, and longer warm spells extend the window over which thermal stratification can strengthen and hypolimnetic anoxia can develop. The return-level table above gives a fixed, long-record reference point (e.g. a 1-in-50-year daily rainfall total) against which to judge whether a given scenario/window’s mean rx1day represents a meaningfully more frequent extreme, rather than reasoning from the 20-30 year scenario windows alone.

Caveats

In addition to the caveats already noted in climate-scenario-workflow.qmd (stationarity of the bias-correction and delta-change transfer functions, the short buoy overlap period, and the known MET_prsttn unit issue) and met-source-comparison.qmd (PC10’s weak day-to-day correlation with the buoy for rainfall, r ~ 0.12 – a long-run climatology/threshold use like this is far less sensitive to that than a day-by-day bias-correction reference would be):

  • The Whakarewarewa gauge is a land station, not over the lake; its absolute rainfall total may differ from true over-lake rainfall even though its long-run climatology is the best available basis for a stable extreme-event threshold at this site.
  • The Gumbel method-of-moments fit is a simple, standard estimator but not the only reasonable choice (e.g. GEV with a shape parameter, or L-moments); treat the return-level table as indicative rather than a design-standard flood estimate.
  • Wind and temperature thresholds still come from only 20 years of ERA5 (1995-2014) – no equivalently long, independent wind/temperature record exists at this site the way it does for rainfall.
  • Extreme indices here are derived from the delta-changed reanalysis sequence, not from the GCM’s own extremes – by construction they inherit the historical record’s actual extreme events (dates, storm tracks, event structure), rescaled by a smooth monthly climate signal, so they cannot represent a genuinely new type of extreme event outside the historical envelope.

## Method

| Index | Definition |
|------------------------------------|------------------------------------|
| `rx1day` | Annual maximum daily rainfall (mm) |
| `heavy_rain_days` | Days/year with daily rainfall above the p95 (wet days only) |
| `extreme_rain_days` | Days/year above the p99 |
| `hot_days` | Days/year with daily maximum air temperature above the historical p95 |
| `wsdi` | Warm-spell duration index: days/year in spells of \>=3 consecutive hot days |
| `max_wind` | Annual maximum hourly wind speed (m/s) |
| `storm_wind_hours` | Hours/year with hourly wind speed above the historical p95 |
| `severe_wind_hours` | Hours/year above the historical p99 |

#| label: extreme-summary-tables
#| echo: false

extremes_df <- tar_read(scenario_extreme_indices_df)
baseline <- tar_read(baseline_extreme_indices)

fmt_index_table <- function(indices, labels, caption) {
  extremes_df |>
    tidyr::pivot_longer(dplyr::all_of(indices), names_to = "index", values_to = "value") |>
    dplyr::group_by(scenario, window, index) |>
    dplyr::summarise(mean = mean(value, na.rm = TRUE),
                     min  = min(value, na.rm = TRUE),
                     max  = max(value, na.rm = TRUE), .groups = "drop") |>
    dplyr::rowwise() |>
    dplyr::mutate(
      base = baseline[[index]],
      pct  = 100 * (mean - base) / base,
      cell = sprintf("%.1f (%.1f–%.1f) [%+.0f%%]", mean, min, max, pct)
    ) |>
    dplyr::ungroup() |>
    dplyr::mutate(index = factor(labels[index], unname(labels))) |>
    dplyr::select(scenario, window, index, cell) |>
    tidyr::pivot_wider(names_from = index, values_from = cell) |>
    dplyr::arrange(window, scenario) |>
    knitr::kable(caption = caption)
}

## Discussion

**Rainfall.** `heavy_rain_days` and `extreme_rain_days` increase modestly and roughly monotonically with emissions scenario and window, consistent with a warmer atmosphere holding more moisture -- but the ensemble range across GCMs is wide relative to the mean shift, and for `rx1day` several GCMs project little change or a slight decrease at some windows (the ratio-based delta-change method rescales the historical daily rainfall sequence rather than restructuring its day-to-day intermittency, so it cannot invent a heavier single-day extreme than proportional scaling of the historical record allows -- see the stationarity caveat in `climate-scenario-workflow.qmd`). Anchoring the rainfall thresholds to the 125-year Whakarewarewa record instead of the 20-year ERA5 window matters here specifically because a 20-year sample gives a noisy p95/p99 estimate -- with a long, independent record the "heavy"/"extreme" definitions themselves are far more stable, so a projected change in frequency is more trustworthy as an actual signal rather than partly an artefact of which 20 years happened to set the threshold.

**Heat.** `hot_days` and `wsdi` (warm-spell duration) show the clearest, most consistent scenario/window gradient of the three groups -- both increase substantially and near-monotonically from `ssp126` to `ssp585` and from the near-term to the end-of-century window, with comparatively narrow GCM spread. This is the expected signature of a monotonic mean-warming delta applied to a fixed threshold: as the whole distribution shifts up, an increasing share of days cross a *fixed, historical* p95 threshold.

**Wind storms.** With the vector-disaggregation fix, wind speed is now shaped directly from the donor's own hourly wind-speed pattern rather than being recomputed from reshaped u/v components, removing the disaggregation-side inflation flagged in the previous version of this page (disaggregated hourly wind no longer runs systematically higher than its own delta-adjusted daily target). The wind-storm indices above should now be read as a direct read on the GCM ensemble's wind delta-change signal, which -- per `gcm_monthly_deltas_df` -- is close to 1 (little change) for most GCM/scenario combinations, with a modest number of outlier models/months driving any larger shifts. Wind remains the index group with the least physical basis for a strong climate-change signal at monthly delta-change resolution, since storm-scale wind events are a synoptic-scale phenomenon that a monthly mean factor is a weak proxy for.

**Practical implication for lake modelling.** Heavy-rain and warm-spell frequency both increase with scenario severity in a way that is directionally robust across the GCM ensemble, and both are levers on lake response that matter beyond the monthly-mean forcing already explored in `climate-scenario-workflow.qmd`: more frequent heavy-rain days translate to more frequent high-inflow/nutrient-loading pulses, and longer warm spells extend the window over which thermal stratification can strengthen and hypolimnetic anoxia can develop. The return-level table above gives a fixed, long-record reference point (e.g. a 1-in-50-year daily rainfall total) against which to judge whether a given scenario/window's mean `rx1day` represents a meaningfully more frequent extreme, rather than reasoning from the 20-30 year scenario windows alone.

## Caveats

In addition to the caveats already noted in `climate-scenario-workflow.qmd` (stationarity of the bias-correction and delta-change transfer functions, the short buoy overlap period, and the known `MET_prsttn` unit issue) and `met-source-comparison.qmd` (PC10's weak day-to-day correlation with the buoy for rainfall, r \~ 0.12 -- a long-run climatology/threshold use like this is far less sensitive to that than a day-by-day bias-correction reference would be):

-   The Whakarewarewa gauge is a land station, not over the lake; its absolute rainfall total may differ from true over-lake rainfall even though its long-run climatology is the best available basis for a stable extreme-event threshold at this site.
-   The Gumbel method-of-moments fit is a simple, standard estimator but not the only reasonable choice (e.g. GEV with a shape parameter, or L-moments); treat the return-level table as indicative rather than a design-standard flood estimate.
-   Wind and temperature thresholds still come from only 20 years of ERA5 (1995-2014) -- no equivalently long, independent wind/temperature record exists at this site the way it does for rainfall.
-   Extreme indices here are derived from the delta-changed *reanalysis* sequence, not from the GCM's own extremes -- by construction they inherit the historical record's actual extreme *events* (dates, storm tracks, event structure), rescaled by a smooth monthly climate signal, so they cannot represent a genuinely new type of extreme event outside the historical envelope.
