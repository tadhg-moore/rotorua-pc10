Climate-Scenario Extreme & Storm-Event Analysis

climate-scenario-workflow.qmd builds scenario_hourly_met – the AEME MET_*-formatted hourly forcing for every GCM x SSP x future-window combination (7 GCMs x 4 SSPs x 3 windows, minus NZESM/ssp585 = 81 branches), by applying each GCM/scenario’s monthly delta-change signal to the bias-corrected daily ERA5 baseline and disaggregating back to hourly against the bias-corrected hourly ERA5 record.

This page uses that same hourly forcing to ask a narrower question: how does the frequency and intensity of extreme/storm-scale events change, not just the monthly-mean climate? Sub-daily (hourly) data matters here because heavy-rain intensity, wind-storm duration and short heatwaves are sub-daily-to-daily phenomena that a monthly delta-change factor alone cannot resolve – they only show up once the delta has been re-expanded to hourly.

This is a rebuild of an earlier version of this page, incorporating two changes made since:

  1. A rainfall unit bug fix. met-source-comparison.qmd caught MET_pprain reading ~1000x too high from ERA5’s raw units; every rainfall figure below is recomputed against the corrected series.
  2. Wind is now disaggregated as a plain scalar, not a vector. metscale::disaggregate_met_to_hourly() was reshaping wind via its u/v vector components, which was found to inflate disaggregated hourly wind speed beyond its own delta-adjusted daily target. apply_gcm_delta_to_baseline() and the scenario_hourly_met target now strip MET_wnduvu/MET_wnduvv/MET_wnddir before disaggregation, so wind speed is shaped directly from the donor’s own wind-speed pattern – direction isn’t needed for this lake model. See the wind section below for the corrected numbers.
  3. Rainfall thresholds now use the 126-year Whakarewarewa rain-gauge record (pc10_climate_met, via BOPRC/PC10 – see met-source-comparison.qmd) instead of the ERA5 reference window (1985-2014, 30 years; originally 1995-2014), and a return-level table is now possible for the first time.
  4. Measured data first. The thresholds and the observed annual indices are built from the Whakarewarewa gauge, the airport and the lake buoy wherever they exist, with the bias-corrected ERA5 series only filling gaps (see Measured data first below). The scenarios themselves are still built from the bias-corrected ERA5 baseline.

Method

For every branch of scenario_hourly_met, and separately for the bias-corrected historical baseline (era5_corrected_hourly, reference years scenario_ref_years, 1985-2014), compute_annual_extreme_indices() (R/summarise_climate_extremes.R) computes one row per calendar year for:

Index Definition
rx1day Annual maximum daily rainfall (mm)
heavy_rain_days Days/year with daily rainfall above the p95 (wet days only)
extreme_rain_days Days/year above the p99
hot_days Days/year with daily maximum air temperature above the historical p95
wsdi Warm-spell duration index: days/year in spells of >=3 consecutive hot days
max_wind Annual maximum hourly wind speed (m/s)
storm_wind_hours Hours/year with hourly wind speed above the historical p95
severe_wind_hours Hours/year above the historical p99
storm_events Number of separate contiguous spells/year above the wind p95 threshold

Thresholds (compute_extreme_thresholds_obs()) are fixed once and applied unchanged to every future branch, so “heavy rain” and “storm wind” mean the same absolute thing in every period – a change in frequency is then a genuine intensification/frequency signal, not an artefact of a moving definition. Wind and temperature thresholds now come from the measured records first (lake buoy, then the airport put on the lake scale) and only use the bias-corrected ERA5 hourly baseline where nothing was measured (see Measured data first below). Rainfall thresholds now come from the 126-year Whakarewarewa daily rain-gauge record (compute_long_rain_thresholds(), on pc10_climate_met) rather than the 20-year ERA5 window: 20 years is thin for estimating a p95/p99 event, and the long, independently-measured record is unaffected by anything in the reanalysis/bias-correction/delta-change chain the scenarios themselves are built from.

Measured data first

The thresholds, and the observed annual indices used for context below, are built from the measured records wherever they exist, with the bias-corrected ERA5 hourly series (era5_corrected_hourly) used only to fill the gaps (build_obs_priority_met(), R/obs_priority_met.R; target met_obs_priority). The order is set variable by variable by which measured record is usable:

  • Rain (daily): the Whakarewarewa rain gauge, then the MetService airport hourly rain (summed to days), then ERA5. The gauge is complete for the whole period, so ERA5 is never needed for rain. The lake buoy’s own rain record is not used (its daily rain is close to zero; see climate-scenario-workflow.qmd).
  • Daily maximum air temperature: the lake buoy (2007 on), then the MetService airport (1993 to 2023) put on the lake scale with the pipeline’s own airport-to-buoy transfer function (niwa_to_buoy_bias_correction, as used to extend the buoy record for the ERA5 correction), then ERA5 before 1993. A day counts as measured only if it has at least 18 hourly values.
  • Hourly wind speed: the lake buoy (2007 on, averaged to the hour), then ERA5. Airport wind is not used: it reads about 35% lower than the lake and the pipeline has no airport-to-buoy transfer for wind.

These are a fixed, historically-grounded reference point – e.g. how the scenario ensemble’s projected rx1day (below) compares to a present-day 1-in-20 or 1-in-50 year event – independent of any GCM or bias-correction choice made elsewhere in this pipeline.

ENSO context: natural year-to-year variability

A change in the frequency of extremes only matters relative to how much they already vary from year to year, and in New Zealand a large part of that variability is the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) and the longer Interdecadal Pacific Oscillation (IPO). ENSO is analysed in full on enso-lake-rotorua.qmd; this section uses the same three ENSO targets to put the scenario changes above in context, over 1975 to the present:

  • soi_long: the 3-month Southern Oscillation Index, the official Stats NZ series from 1990 and the NOAA SOI rescaled to it before 1990, with phases (El Niño at most -1, La Niña at least +1);
  • statsnz_soi: the official Stats NZ series and phases alone (1990 on), which soi_long reproduces;
  • enso_indices: the NOAA SOI and Oceanic Niño Index (ONI), the IPO tripole index and the Southern Annular Mode.

The annual extreme indices come from the measured-first record (obs_annual_extremes: gauge, airport and buoy where they exist, bias-corrected ERA5 for the gaps; see above), for every complete calendar year 1975 to 2025.

What the record shows. Over 1975 to 2025 (5 El Niño, 38 neutral and 8 La Niña seasons) ENSO explains only a small part of the year-to-year spread in these indices. The strongest link to the October to March SOI is for hot days (Spearman r = -0.28, p = 0.047); of the 27 correlations in the table (9 indices against the SOI, ONI and IPO), 1 fall below p = 0.05, about what chance alone would give. For heavy-rain days, La Niña years have 1.1 more days than El Niño years against a natural spread of 3.4 days (p = 0.51). With about 50 years and only a handful of El Niño and La Niña seasons the tests are weak, so treat the table as an indication of the size of the effect, not a firm result. This is consistent with enso-lake-rotorua.qmd, where the meteorological ENSO effect is within the spread of neutral years and clearer for rainfall totals than for single extremes.

Reading the scenario changes against it. The last table puts the scenario shifts on the same scale as the natural spread. A shift of one standard deviation or more is outside what ENSO, the IPO or ordinary year-to-year variability produces in the present climate; a shift well below one standard deviation would be hard to tell from a run of El Niño or La Niña years in any single decade. The CMIP6 data used here are free-running and carry no ENSO phase (see enso-lake-rotorua.qmd), so the scenarios shift the mean climate only and do not change how often El Niño or La Niña years occur; a lake run through them should still be read against ENSO variability of about the size shown here.

Discussion

Rainfall. heavy_rain_days and extreme_rain_days show no consistent change with emissions scenario or window: the ensemble means move between roughly -30% and +10% of the baseline with no monotonic gradient, and rx1day between about -5% and +25%, with a GCM range that is wide relative to those shifts (so several GCMs project a decrease in some windows). The ratio-based delta-change method rescales the historical daily rainfall sequence rather than restructuring its day-to-day intermittency, so it cannot invent a heavier single-day extreme than proportional scaling of the historical record allows (see the stationarity caveat in climate-scenario-workflow.qmd). The changes should therefore be read as small compared with the natural spread (see the ENSO section above, where the scenario shift in rain extremes is a fraction of one standard deviation). Anchoring the rainfall thresholds to the 126-year Whakarewarewa record instead of the 30-year ERA5 window matters because a 30-year sample gives a noisy p95/p99 estimate; with a long, independent record the “heavy”/“extreme” definitions are far more stable, so a projected change in frequency is more trustworthy as an actual signal. One caveat applies to the rainfall baseline: bias-corrected ERA5 has more rain extremes than the gauge over the same years (see Measured data first), so the percentage changes are measured against a baseline that is itself too extreme.

Heat. hot_days and wsdi (warm-spell duration) show the clearest, most consistent scenario/window gradient: both increase substantially and near-monotonically from ssp126 to ssp585 and from the near-term to the end-of-century window, with comparatively narrow GCM spread, and the shifts are several times the natural year-to-year spread. This is the expected signature of a monotonic mean-warming delta applied to a fixed threshold: as the whole distribution shifts up, an increasing share of days cross a fixed, historical p95 threshold. The percentage increases are measured against the 1985-2014 baseline mean (about 20 hot days a year) while every scenario branch covers the whole 1972-2026 record, whose bias-corrected ERA5 mean over the complete years 1973-2025 is higher (about 23 hot days, because the 2010s and 2020s were already warmer); against the full-record mean the near-term percentage increase is smaller.

Wind storms. With the vector-disaggregation fix, wind speed is now shaped directly from the donor’s own hourly wind-speed pattern rather than being recomputed from reshaped u/v components, removing the disaggregation-side inflation flagged in the previous version of this page (disaggregated hourly wind no longer runs systematically higher than its own delta-adjusted daily target). The wind-storm indices above should now be read as a direct read on the GCM ensemble’s wind delta-change signal, which – per gcm_monthly_deltas_df – is close to 1 (little change) for most GCM/scenario combinations, with a modest number of outlier models/months driving any larger shifts. In the rebuilt ensemble the wind-storm indices change little in the near term and fall in the later windows, most under ssp585 (storm-wind hours about -40% by 2071-2100), driven by the GCM wind deltas rather than by any change in event structure. Wind remains the index group with the least physical basis for a strong climate-change signal at monthly delta-change resolution, since storm-scale wind events are a synoptic-scale phenomenon that a monthly mean factor is a weak proxy for.

Practical implication for lake modelling. Warm-spell frequency increases with scenario severity in a way that is directionally robust across the GCM ensemble, whereas heavy-rain frequency does not change consistently. Both are levers on lake response that matter beyond the monthly-mean forcing already explored in climate-scenario-workflow.qmd: heavy-rain days drive high-inflow/nutrient-loading pulses (no clear change here), and longer warm spells extend the window over which thermal stratification can strengthen and hypolimnetic anoxia can develop. The return-level table above gives a fixed, long-record reference point (e.g. a 1-in-50-year daily rainfall total) against which to judge whether a given scenario/window’s mean rx1day represents a meaningfully more frequent extreme, rather than reasoning from the 20-30 year scenario windows alone.

Caveats

In addition to the caveats already noted in climate-scenario-workflow.qmd (stationarity of the bias-correction and delta-change transfer functions, the short buoy overlap period, and the known MET_prsttn unit issue) and met-source-comparison.qmd (PC10’s weak day-to-day correlation with the buoy for rainfall, r ~ 0.12 – a long-run climatology/threshold use like this is far less sensitive to that than a day-by-day bias-correction reference would be):

  • The Whakarewarewa gauge is a land station, not over the lake; its absolute rainfall total may differ from true over-lake rainfall even though its long-run climatology is the best available basis for a stable extreme-event threshold at this site.
  • The Gumbel method-of-moments fit is a simple, standard estimator but not the only reasonable choice (e.g. GEV with a shape parameter, or L-moments); treat the return-level table as indicative rather than a design-standard flood estimate.
  • The scenario branches cover the whole 1972-2026 bias-corrected record, but the baseline for the percentage changes is the 1985-2014 mean, so the two are not over the same years; for hot days the full-record baseline is about 17% higher (22.8 vs 19.6 days a year).
  • Wind and temperature thresholds use the lake buoy (2007 on) and the airport on the lake scale (1993 to 2006), with bias-corrected ERA5 only before 1993 and for gaps; the wind record is therefore mostly ERA5 before 2007, and airport wind cannot stand in for it.
  • Extreme indices here are derived from the delta-changed reanalysis sequence, not from the GCM’s own extremes – by construction they inherit the historical record’s actual extreme events (dates, storm tracks, event structure), rescaled by a smooth monthly climate signal, so they cannot represent a genuinely new type of extreme event outside the historical envelope.

[`climate-scenario-workflow.qmd`](climate-scenario-workflow.qmd) builds `scenario_hourly_met` -- the AEME `MET_*`-formatted hourly forcing for every GCM x SSP x future-window combination (7 GCMs x 4 SSPs x 3 windows, minus `NZESM`/`ssp585` = 81 branches), by applying each GCM/scenario's monthly delta-change signal to the bias-corrected daily ERA5 baseline and disaggregating back to hourly against the bias-corrected hourly ERA5 record.

This page uses that same hourly forcing to ask a narrower question: **how does the frequency and intensity of extreme/storm-scale events change**, not just the monthly-mean climate? Sub-daily (hourly) data matters here because heavy-rain intensity, wind-storm duration and short heatwaves are sub-daily-to-daily phenomena that a monthly delta-change factor alone cannot resolve -- they only show up once the delta has been re-expanded to hourly.

This is a rebuild of an earlier version of this page, incorporating two changes made since:

1.  **A rainfall unit bug fix.** [`met-source-comparison.qmd`](met-source-comparison.qmd) caught `MET_pprain` reading \~1000x too high from ERA5's raw units; every rainfall figure below is recomputed against the corrected series.
2.  **Wind is now disaggregated as a plain scalar, not a vector.** `metscale::disaggregate_met_to_hourly()` was reshaping wind via its u/v vector components, which was found to inflate disaggregated hourly wind speed beyond its own delta-adjusted daily target. `apply_gcm_delta_to_baseline()` and the `scenario_hourly_met` target now strip `MET_wnduvu`/`MET_wnduvv`/`MET_wnddir` before disaggregation, so wind speed is shaped directly from the donor's own wind-speed pattern -- direction isn't needed for this lake model. See the wind section below for the corrected numbers.
3.  **Rainfall thresholds now use the 126-year Whakarewarewa rain-gauge record** (`pc10_climate_met`, via BOPRC/PC10 -- see [`met-source-comparison.qmd`](met-source-comparison.qmd)) instead of the ERA5 reference window (1985-2014, 30 years; originally 1995-2014), and a return-level table is now possible for the first time.
4.  **Measured data first.** The thresholds and the observed annual indices are built from the Whakarewarewa gauge, the airport and the lake buoy wherever they exist, with the bias-corrected ERA5 series only filling gaps (see *Measured data first* below). The scenarios themselves are still built from the bias-corrected ERA5 baseline.

## Method

For every branch of `scenario_hourly_met`, and separately for the bias-corrected historical baseline (`era5_corrected_hourly`, reference years `scenario_ref_years`, 1985-2014), `compute_annual_extreme_indices()` ([R/summarise_climate_extremes.R](../R/summarise_climate_extremes.R)) computes one row per calendar year for:

| Index | Definition |
|------------------------------------|------------------------------------|
| `rx1day` | Annual maximum daily rainfall (mm) |
| `heavy_rain_days` | Days/year with daily rainfall above the p95 (wet days only) |
| `extreme_rain_days` | Days/year above the p99 |
| `hot_days` | Days/year with daily maximum air temperature above the historical p95 |
| `wsdi` | Warm-spell duration index: days/year in spells of \>=3 consecutive hot days |
| `max_wind` | Annual maximum hourly wind speed (m/s) |
| `storm_wind_hours` | Hours/year with hourly wind speed above the historical p95 |
| `severe_wind_hours` | Hours/year above the historical p99 |
| `storm_events` | Number of separate contiguous spells/year above the wind p95 threshold |

Thresholds (`compute_extreme_thresholds_obs()`) are fixed **once** and applied unchanged to every future branch, so "heavy rain" and "storm wind" mean the same absolute thing in every period -- a change in frequency is then a genuine intensification/frequency signal, not an artefact of a moving definition. Wind and temperature thresholds now come from the measured records first (lake buoy, then the airport put on the lake scale) and only use the bias-corrected ERA5 hourly baseline where nothing was measured (see *Measured data first* below). **Rainfall thresholds now come from the 126-year Whakarewarewa daily rain-gauge record** (`compute_long_rain_thresholds()`, on `pc10_climate_met`) rather than the 20-year ERA5 window: 20 years is thin for estimating a p95/p99 event, and the long, independently-measured record is unaffected by anything in the reanalysis/bias-correction/delta-change chain the scenarios themselves are built from.

### Measured data first

The thresholds, and the observed annual indices used for context below, are built from the **measured records wherever they exist**, with the bias-corrected ERA5 hourly series (`era5_corrected_hourly`) used only to fill the gaps (`build_obs_priority_met()`, [R/obs_priority_met.R](../R/obs_priority_met.R); target `met_obs_priority`). The order is set variable by variable by which measured record is usable:

-   **Rain (daily):** the Whakarewarewa rain gauge, then the MetService airport hourly rain (summed to days), then ERA5. The gauge is complete for the whole period, so ERA5 is never needed for rain. The lake buoy's own rain record is not used (its daily rain is close to zero; see `climate-scenario-workflow.qmd`).
-   **Daily maximum air temperature:** the lake buoy (2007 on), then the MetService airport (1993 to 2023) put on the lake scale with the pipeline's own airport-to-buoy transfer function (`niwa_to_buoy_bias_correction`, as used to extend the buoy record for the ERA5 correction), then ERA5 before 1993. A day counts as measured only if it has at least 18 hourly values.
-   **Hourly wind speed:** the lake buoy (2007 on, averaged to the hour), then ERA5. Airport wind is not used: it reads about 35% lower than the lake and the pipeline has no airport-to-buoy transfer for wind.

## ENSO context: natural year-to-year variability

A change in the frequency of extremes only matters relative to how much they already vary from year to year, and in New Zealand a large part of that variability is the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) and the longer Interdecadal Pacific Oscillation (IPO). ENSO is analysed in full on [`enso-lake-rotorua.qmd`](enso-lake-rotorua.qmd); this section uses the same three ENSO targets to put the scenario changes above in context, over 1975 to the present:

-   `soi_long`: the 3-month Southern Oscillation Index, the official [Stats NZ](https://www.stats.govt.nz/indicators/el-nino-southern-oscillation-data-to-2025/) series from 1990 and the NOAA SOI rescaled to it before 1990, with phases (El Niño at most -1, La Niña at least +1);
-   `statsnz_soi`: the official Stats NZ series and phases alone (1990 on), which `soi_long` reproduces;
-   `enso_indices`: the NOAA SOI and Oceanic Niño Index (ONI), the IPO tripole index and the Southern Annular Mode.

The annual extreme indices come from the **measured-first record** (`obs_annual_extremes`: gauge, airport and buoy where they exist, bias-corrected ERA5 for the gaps; see above), for every complete calendar year 1975 to 2025.

**Reading the scenario changes against it.** The last table puts the scenario shifts on the same scale as the natural spread. A shift of one standard deviation or more is outside what ENSO, the IPO or ordinary year-to-year variability produces in the present climate; a shift well below one standard deviation would be hard to tell from a run of El Niño or La Niña years in any single decade. The CMIP6 data used here are free-running and carry no ENSO phase (see `enso-lake-rotorua.qmd`), so the scenarios shift the *mean* climate only and do not change how often El Niño or La Niña years occur; a lake run through them should still be read against ENSO variability of about the size shown here.

## Discussion

**Rainfall.** `heavy_rain_days` and `extreme_rain_days` show **no consistent change** with emissions scenario or window: the ensemble means move between roughly -30% and +10% of the baseline with no monotonic gradient, and `rx1day` between about -5% and +25%, with a GCM range that is wide relative to those shifts (so several GCMs project a decrease in some windows). The ratio-based delta-change method rescales the historical daily rainfall sequence rather than restructuring its day-to-day intermittency, so it cannot invent a heavier single-day extreme than proportional scaling of the historical record allows (see the stationarity caveat in `climate-scenario-workflow.qmd`). The changes should therefore be read as small compared with the natural spread (see the ENSO section above, where the scenario shift in rain extremes is a fraction of one standard deviation). Anchoring the rainfall thresholds to the 126-year Whakarewarewa record instead of the 30-year ERA5 window matters because a 30-year sample gives a noisy p95/p99 estimate; with a long, independent record the "heavy"/"extreme" definitions are far more stable, so a projected change in frequency is more trustworthy as an actual signal. One caveat applies to the rainfall baseline: bias-corrected ERA5 has more rain extremes than the gauge over the same years (see *Measured data first*), so the percentage changes are measured against a baseline that is itself too extreme.

**Heat.** `hot_days` and `wsdi` (warm-spell duration) show the clearest, most consistent scenario/window gradient: both increase substantially and near-monotonically from `ssp126` to `ssp585` and from the near-term to the end-of-century window, with comparatively narrow GCM spread, and the shifts are several times the natural year-to-year spread. This is the expected signature of a monotonic mean-warming delta applied to a fixed threshold: as the whole distribution shifts up, an increasing share of days cross a *fixed, historical* p95 threshold. The percentage increases are measured against the 1985-2014 baseline mean (about 20 hot days a year) while every scenario branch covers the whole 1972-2026 record, whose bias-corrected ERA5 mean over the complete years 1973-2025 is higher (about 23 hot days, because the 2010s and 2020s were already warmer); against the full-record mean the near-term percentage increase is smaller.

**Wind storms.** With the vector-disaggregation fix, wind speed is now shaped directly from the donor's own hourly wind-speed pattern rather than being recomputed from reshaped u/v components, removing the disaggregation-side inflation flagged in the previous version of this page (disaggregated hourly wind no longer runs systematically higher than its own delta-adjusted daily target). The wind-storm indices above should now be read as a direct read on the GCM ensemble's wind delta-change signal, which -- per `gcm_monthly_deltas_df` -- is close to 1 (little change) for most GCM/scenario combinations, with a modest number of outlier models/months driving any larger shifts. In the rebuilt ensemble the wind-storm indices change little in the near term and fall in the later windows, most under `ssp585` (storm-wind hours about -40% by 2071-2100), driven by the GCM wind deltas rather than by any change in event structure. Wind remains the index group with the least physical basis for a strong climate-change signal at monthly delta-change resolution, since storm-scale wind events are a synoptic-scale phenomenon that a monthly mean factor is a weak proxy for.

**Practical implication for lake modelling.** Warm-spell frequency increases with scenario severity in a way that is directionally robust across the GCM ensemble, whereas heavy-rain frequency does not change consistently. Both are levers on lake response that matter beyond the monthly-mean forcing already explored in `climate-scenario-workflow.qmd`: heavy-rain days drive high-inflow/nutrient-loading pulses (no clear change here), and longer warm spells extend the window over which thermal stratification can strengthen and hypolimnetic anoxia can develop. The return-level table above gives a fixed, long-record reference point (e.g. a 1-in-50-year daily rainfall total) against which to judge whether a given scenario/window's mean `rx1day` represents a meaningfully more frequent extreme, rather than reasoning from the 20-30 year scenario windows alone.

## Caveats

In addition to the caveats already noted in `climate-scenario-workflow.qmd` (stationarity of the bias-correction and delta-change transfer functions, the short buoy overlap period, and the known `MET_prsttn` unit issue) and `met-source-comparison.qmd` (PC10's weak day-to-day correlation with the buoy for rainfall, r \~ 0.12 -- a long-run climatology/threshold use like this is far less sensitive to that than a day-by-day bias-correction reference would be):

-   The Whakarewarewa gauge is a land station, not over the lake; its absolute rainfall total may differ from true over-lake rainfall even though its long-run climatology is the best available basis for a stable extreme-event threshold at this site.
-   The Gumbel method-of-moments fit is a simple, standard estimator but not the only reasonable choice (e.g. GEV with a shape parameter, or L-moments); treat the return-level table as indicative rather than a design-standard flood estimate.
-   The scenario branches cover the whole 1972-2026 bias-corrected record, but the baseline for the percentage changes is the 1985-2014 mean, so the two are not over the same years; for hot days the full-record baseline is about 17% higher (22.8 vs 19.6 days a year).
-   Wind and temperature thresholds use the lake buoy (2007 on) and the airport on the lake scale (1993 to 2006), with bias-corrected ERA5 only before 1993 and for gaps; the wind record is therefore mostly ERA5 before 2007, and airport wind cannot stand in for it.
-   Extreme indices here are derived from the delta-changed *reanalysis* sequence, not from the GCM's own extremes -- by construction they inherit the historical record's actual extreme *events* (dates, storm tracks, event structure), rescaled by a smooth monthly climate signal, so they cannot represent a genuinely new type of extreme event outside the historical envelope.
