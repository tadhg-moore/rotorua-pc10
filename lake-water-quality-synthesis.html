Lake Rotorua water quality: state, trends and drivers

A synthesis of lake chemistry, CTD and buoy profiles, meteorology, alum dosing, river water quality and the 2026 PC10 loads

Author

Tadhg Moore

Published

November 12, 2026

1 Key findings

The lake today. Lake Rotorua is a eutrophic, polymictic lake.

  • The 3-year mean TLI3 for 2024–2026 is 4.39 (hydrological year 2026).
    • This is above the target of 4.2.
    • It is well below the 2004–2006 peak of 5.04.
    • It is higher than the 2014–2020 low of 4.15.
  • Integrated surface-water means for 2023–2026: TP ≈ 19 mg/m³, TN ≈ 354 mg/m³, chl-a ≈ 12.7 mg/m³.

Three phases in the record.

  1. Early-2000s eutrophic peak.
    • TP ≈ 41, TN ≈ 456, chl-a ≈ 23 mg/m³ (2001–2006).
    • Frequent bottom-water anoxia, with summer bottom NH₄ and DRP above 400 and 50 mg/m³.
  2. Alum-era recovery, 2007 to about 2012.
    • TP fell by about half, to ≈ 17 mg/m³ (2012–2020).
    • TN fell to ≈ 319 mg/m³ and chl-a roughly halved.
    • The step changes in TP, DRP and particulate P are detected in 2007–2010. They follow Utuhina dosing (from June 2006) and Puarenga dosing (from January 2010).
  3. A partial reversal since about 2016. Over the last 10 years TP, TN and chl-a show significant increasing trends at both sites, and Secchi depth is decreasing at Site 5. Two things stand out:
    • The 2020–2022 TP excursion. Mean TP was ≈ 30 mg/m³ in 2021–2022. Bottom TP reached 160 mg/m³ and DRP 110–120 mg/m³ in summer 2021/22. Three things coincided:
      • a near-complete pause in Puarenga dosing (Aug 2018 to Nov 2020);
      • the strongest lake heatwave and the longest buoy-recorded stratification (70 days, Nov 2021 – Jan 2022);
      • two low-load years (external TP ≈ 28–29 t/y).
      TP returned to about 19 mg/m³ once dosing resumed and the very wet 2023 brought a well-mixed year.
    • Nitrogen is rising. TN rose from about 300 to about 360 mg/m³ after 2021. This follows the high-flow 2023 and 2024 hydrological years, when modelled external TN loads were 814 and 706 t/y against the target of 435 t/y. In-lake NH₄ and NOx are also higher than in the 2010s.

Drivers.

  • Alum is the strongest single correlate of lake TP and TLI (ρ ≈ −0.8). It is confounded with the time trend, but the 2019–2021 dosing pause acts as a natural experiment that supports a causal link.
  • Internal loading. Year-to-year changes in summer air temperature track bottom-water oxygen and NH₄ accumulation. Hot, calm summers produce long stratification, anoxia and internal P and N release: 2008/09, 2010/11, 2014/15, 2017/18, 2021/22 and 2023/24.
  • Internal release rates. The bottom layer below the thermocline (typically 9–12 m deep in summer) is only about 5 m thick on average. During stratified periods:
    • it loses oxygen at about 1.5 g O₂/m²/d (the depletion rate rises about 5 % per °C);
    • it gains NH₄ at about 25–80 mg N/m²/d and DRP at about 2–5 mg P/m²/d (lower bounds).
    These rates suggest roughly 3–15 t of DRP and 30–230 t of NH₄-N are released each summer from the deep sediments alone (Section 5).
  • External loads. Wet years raise external loads (2011–2013, 2017–2018, 2023–2024) but tend to flush and mix the lake, so they reduce peak bottom DRP.
  • Warming. The bias-corrected ERA5 record warms by about 1 °C over 1977–2025. Air-heatwave days have increased from typically 5–20 per year before 2016 to mostly 25–55 per year since then.

Data-quality caveats. These affect the interpretation of trends.

  • The lake DRP, NH₄, NOx and TP records show changes in reporting resolution: zeros and negatives in 2015–2020, non-integer TP in 2015–2018, and finer decimals from 2021. These point to laboratory or method changes, so the “increasing DRP” trend over the last 10 years is not reliable.
  • A synchronous TP rise and fall (2011–2019) across all streams, including spring-fed ones, is more likely analytical than catchment-driven.
  • See Section 3.6.

2 Data

Notes on the data.

  • Hydrological years. Lake summaries use July–June hydrological years, named by the year they end. For example, 2022 is July 2021 to June 2022, and its summer is December 2021 to February 2022.
  • INT samples are depth-integrated over the epilimnion (0 m to roughly 6–14 m).
  • BOT samples are taken about 1 m above the sediment, at 18–22 m.
  • Trend tests are seasonal Mann–Kendall tests on monthly medians with Sen slopes. They are not corrected for serial correlation, so p-values are optimistic.
  • Change points are binary segmentation of deseasonalised monthly anomalies, with a conservative penalty.

Known caveats.

  • Site codes. The alkalinity file uses EL805168 for Site 5, against EL802148 here.
  • Ōhau outlet record. It may contain a lab or method change, which is not yet confirmed (see the Ōhau page).
  • Buoy met. Units changed on 2023-10-23 (fixed in the pipeline), and buoy RH drifts.
  • Puarenga river site. The routine site (Puarenga at FRI, EK598179) is upstream of the alum plant, so it does not show the effect of dosing.
  • Utuhina river site. The Utuhina at Lake Rd site is downstream of its plant.

3 In-lake nutrient chemistry

3.1 Long-term patterns

Phosphorus.

  • Before alum (1990s–2006). Integrated TP was mostly 30–48 mg/m³. A high 1993/94 (≈ 80 mg/m³) reflects very few samples in that year.
  • Fall with dosing. TP fell from about 40 mg/m³ in 2006 to 15–20 mg/m³ from 2011/12.
  • Composition. DRP dropped from 5–15 mg/m³ to mostly ≤ 2 mg/m³ in the surface water. Most of the remaining TP is particulate or organic (TP − DRP), which also halved, from about 30 to 15 mg/m³.
  • 2021–2022. TP was clearly elevated (27–33 mg/m³), with DRP rising to 4–8 mg/m³. It then fell back to 18–21 mg/m³ in 2023–2026.

Nitrogen.

  • Peak. TN was highest in 2000–2007 (≈ 450–575 mg/m³ at Site 2).
  • Step down. It stepped down around 2009/10 (change point Aug 2009 at Site 2, Feb 2008 at Site 5) to about 300 mg/m³ in 2011–2021. The drop is almost all organic N (TKN − NH₄), which follows the fall in phytoplankton biomass.
  • Since 2022. TN has risen to 340–365 mg/m³.
  • Inorganic N is more variable:
    • NH₄ peaks in years of strong internal loading.
    • NOx-N was close to detection before 2011 and much higher afterwards: years such as 2012–2013, 2017–2018 and 2023–2024 have annual means of 40–140 mg/m³.
    • The NOx step in 2011 appears at both lake sites and, in part, in several streams. It may be partly analytical (Section 3.6), but high NOx after winter mixing is a real feature of the lake.

Chlorophyll-a and clarity.

  • Chl-a peaked in 2001–2007 (20–30 mg/m³ annual means) and fell to 8–13 mg/m³ after 2012.
  • Secchi depth improved from about 2.5 to about 3 m.
  • Last decade. Both have edged back towards poorer conditions: chl-a 12–16 mg/m³ and Secchi 2.4–2.5 m in 2026.

3.2 Seasonal cycle and how it has changed

The seasonal cycle.

  • Late summer and autumn (Feb–May). After stratification breaks down, nutrients released from the sediment are mixed into the surface water. TP, DRP and NH₄ peak at this time.
  • Winter and spring (Jul–Oct). NOx-N peaks, as the NH₄ is nitrified and the lake stays mixed.

How the cycle has changed.

  • TP and DRP. The late-summer and autumn TP peak (March–April) is about half of its pre-alum size (about 27 against 56 mg/m³), and similar to 2006–2016. The large autumn DRP peak of the pre-alum era (about 20 mg/m³ in March) has disappeared.
  • Ammonium. The autumn NH₄ peak is now the largest of the three periods (median about 50 mg/m³ in April).
  • Nitrate. The winter NOx peak (July–October, about 60–100 mg/m³) is much larger than before.
  • Nitrogen overall. Together these suggest that N released from the sediment in summer is now mixed and nitrified in winter, rather than taken up by phytoplankton.
  • Chl-a. It no longer shows the large summer–autumn maxima of the early 2000s, and in the last decade the seasonal range is only about 8–14 mg/m³.

3.3 Trends

What the trends show.

  • Whole record. TP, DRP, particulate P, TN, organic N and chl-a are all decreasing, and Secchi is increasing. This reflects the alum-era recovery.
  • Last decade (2016–2026). The direction has reversed:
    • TP is increasing by about 2 %/yr at both sites.
    • TN is increasing by about 1.5–1.8 %/yr.
    • Chl-a is increasing by about 3–4 %/yr.
    • Secchi depth is decreasing at Site 5.
  • DRP over the last decade. The significant DRP increase over 2016–2026 is driven by the many zero values reported in 2015–2019 (Section 3.6). It should not be read as a real increase.
  • TN:TP. The ratio has risen from about 25 to about 40 (molar). P limitation has strengthened under alum dosing.

3.4 Bottom waters and internal loading

Internal loading remains active.

  • Years with strong bottom-water accumulation (two-site mean of the annual bottom maxima: DRP above 50 mg/m³ and NH₄ above 400 mg/m³) are 2004, 2010, 2015, 2022 and 2025. 2016, 2021 and 2024 are close to these thresholds.
  • Years with little accumulation are 2007, 2012–2014, 2017–2019, 2023 and 2026.
  • Bottom-water trends:
    • Bottom TP and DRP have declined over the whole record, but show no decline over the last decade.
    • Bottom NH₄ has increased over the alum era at both sites (+1.8 to +4.6 %/yr).
  • So alum has suppressed the P released from the sediment, but it has not removed it, and N release continues.
  • The seasonal timing, the thermal controls and estimates of the release rates are in Section 5.

3.5 Trophic Level Index

The TLI trajectory.

  • Peak. The 3-year TLI3 peaked at 5.0 in 2004–2006.
  • Fall. It fell steadily with dosing, and from 2013 to 2021 it stayed close to the target (4.15–4.28).
  • Recent years. Since 2022 it has stayed at 4.3–4.4.
    • 2022 was the TP excursion year.
    • In 2024–2026 TN is higher and chl-a is creeping up.
    • 2026 has the highest annual chl-a (≈ 16 mg/m³) since 2009.

3.6 Data quality and laboratory changes

Lake record. The lake record has at least three visible reporting regimes:

  1. To about 2014. Values are whole numbers in mg/m³, with detection limits of 0.5–1 mg/m³.
  2. 2015–2020. Many DRP values are reported as 0, with occasional −1 values in DRP, NOx and NH₄. TP is reported as non-integer values (e.g. 14.159744 and 16.113176), which look like conversions from a different unit or a different instrument.
  3. From 2021. All nutrients are reported to 0.1 mg/m³.

Effects on trends.

  • Trends in low-concentration surface DRP, NH₄ and NOx across these boundaries are unreliable.
  • TP and TN trends are less affected, because concentrations are far above detection.

River record.

  • TP at every stream, including the spring-fed Hamurana and Awahou streams, rises together from about 2011 and falls from about 2019 (Figure 26).
  • Spring-fed streams have stable, mainly geological P, so a shared step of about 20–30 % is much more likely to be analytical than a change in the catchment.
  • River DRP is also high at most sites in 1993–1995 and 2003–2008, which may be a further reporting boundary.
  • This matches the open question about a lab or method change in the Ōhau outlet record (labels change from “Labstar – Legacy” to “Routine” in July 2015, and grade codes change around 2020/21).
  • Recommendation: confirm the BOPRC lab and method history (lab provider, DRP/TP methods, detection limits, censoring rules) before any trend in DRP or river TP is used in reporting or calibration.

4.2 Light, chlorophyll and the new buoy sensors

Light attenuation. Kd from the CTD PAR profiles shifts in steps: about 0.35 m⁻¹ in 2003–2008, about 0.9 m⁻¹ in 2010–2016, back to about 0.35 m⁻¹ in 2020–2022, then rising again. Secchi depth improved over 2010–2016, when Kd suggests the water became darker, so these steps are more likely changes in the PAR sensor or in how the profiles were processed. Kd should not be used for trend analysis without checking the instrument history.

New buoy, 2022–2026.

  • Surface chlorophyll fluorescence shows bloom episodes in late summer and autumn, and higher values in 2026.
  • Sensor caveats:
    • Conductance. Specific conductance has a saw-tooth pattern of slow drift and abrupt steps, which matches sensor drift and recalibration at servicing rather than changes in the lake.
    • Chlorophyll. Fluorescence decays to a flat, low level through 2024–2025, which is typical of fouling.
    • pH. Surface pH drifted from about 7.5 (2022–2023) to about 6.7 (2025). This needs checking against discrete pH (see the pH page) before it is interpreted.
    • CTD chlorophyll. The CTD chlorophyll series also drops sharply in 2020–2021, at the same time as the drop in Kd, which suggests a sensor or calibration change rather than a real fall.
  • The new-buoy data are most reliable for temperature and DO. They show bottom anoxia only in the summers of 2023/24 and 2024/25.

4.3 Lake heatwaves

The strongest lake heatwaves.

  • December 2017 (20 days, +3.9 °C)
  • Christmas 2021 to January 2022 (19 days, “strong”)
  • November 2025
  • January 2024 and January 2015 (both coinciding with long anoxic periods)

The highest surface temperatures in the buoy record are 24–24.7 °C, in summer 2015/16, 2017/18, 2021/22 and 2023/24.

5 Internal loading

Lake Rotorua is polymictic. It does not stratify through the whole summer like a deep monomictic lake. Instead it stratifies for days to weeks at a time between about October and March, and wind events mix it in between. Each stratified period isolates a thin bottom layer, roughly 3–8 m thick, over the deep sediments. That layer loses oxygen quickly and gains NH₄ and DRP released from the sediment. The next mixing event carries those nutrients into the surface water. This section uses the temperature profiles (buoys and CTD), the bottom-water chemistry and the lake hypsography to describe this cycle and to estimate the release rates.

5.1 Seasonal cycle of stratification

When the lake stratifies.

  • Stratification starts to develop in October and November.
  • It is most frequent and strongest from December to February. On about half of all summer days the surface is at least 1 °C warmer than the bottom, and Schmidt stability on calm, hot days exceeds 100–300 J/m².
  • It collapses in March as the lake cools. From April to August the lake is fully mixed.
  • Thermocline depth. On stratified days the thermocline is typically 9–12 m deep (monthly medians; interquartile range about 7–13 m). The isolated bottom layer therefore covers roughly a third to half of the lake bed, because the hypsograph is steep below 10 m.
  • The bottom layer is thin. Its mean thickness (hypolimnion volume divided by its sediment area) is only about 4–6 m. A thin layer over a large sediment area is why oxygen falls so fast: below 2 g/m³ within 1–3 weeks of the onset of stratification.

5.2 Stratified periods and the thermocline, summer by summer

Summer by summer. The summers differ mainly in how long the stratified periods last and how often wind breaks them up.

  • Long stratified periods with prolonged bottom anoxia: 2007/08, 2008/09, 2014/15, 2015/16, 2017/18 and 2023/24. In these summers the thermocline deepens steadily, by about 1 m a week, from 6–9 m to 12–16 m as the period goes on.
  • Long stratification without bottom-DO data: 2019/20 and 2021/22 (grey points). The bottom chemistry shows strong accumulation in both.
  • Short stratified periods separated by frequent mixing, with little anoxia: 2011/12, 2012/13, 2013/14, 2016/17, 2022/23 and 2025/26.
  • Missing: the 2009/10 and 2010/11 summers are largely missing from the buoy record.
  • New profiler (from 2022). Thermocline depths are similar to the old buoy’s: a median of about 10 m in November–December and about 13 m in January.

5.3 Seasonal cycle of bottom-water nutrients

The internal-loading signal follows stratification closely.

  • The bottom-water excess over the surface water (BOT − INT) is near zero from April to September.
  • It builds from November or December and peaks in January–February, which is the timing of the longest stratified, anoxic periods.
  • It collapses again in March as the stratification breaks down. In the integrated surface samples, the released NH₄ and P then appear as the March–May peaks in Figure 2.

Before and after alum.

  • Before alum, the median December–March bottom DRP excess was 12–18 mg/m³.
  • Since 2006, it has been 0–5 mg/m³.
  • NH₄ shows no such fall. Its summer bottom excess in 2016–2026 (up to about 210 mg/m³ in January) is larger than before alum, and it starts earlier, from November.
  • TP. The TP excess in 2016–2026 is again larger than in 2006–2016.
  • Reading. This pattern supports the view that alum caps the release of P from the sediment, but does not reduce the mineralisation of organic matter that supplies N.

5.4 Stratification strength and internal loading, year by year

Year-to-year links between stratification and bottom concentrations. These use the 17 hydrological years with buoy coverage.

  • NH₄:
    • against mean summer Schmidt stability, ρ = 0.39, p = 0.12
    • against stratified days, ρ = 0.10, p = 0.69
  • DRP:
    • against Schmidt stability, ρ = 0.37, p = 0.15
    • against stratified days, ρ = 0.17, p = 0.52

How to read these.

  • The relationships are weakly positive (ρ ≈ 0.4 with Schmidt stability, p ≈ 0.1) and essentially absent for the count of stratified days. Monthly bottom samples catch the peak of each event only by chance, and the strength of stratification matters more than its duration.
  • For DRP, the alum era shifts the response downwards. For the same stratification, post-2010 years mostly have lower bottom DRP, with the exceptions of 2015, 2021, 2022 and 2025.
  • 2021/22 stands out as high stratification combined with very high DRP, in the year the Puarenga dosing was paused.

5.5 Estimating release rates (fluxes)

Two independent estimates are possible from the monitoring data. Both are compared with the sediment-chamber fluxes measured in the lake by Burger et al. (2007) and with the values in the model’s aed.nml.

1. Hypolimnetic accumulation (NH₄, DRP, TP).

  • Which samples are used. Pairs of consecutive monthly samples at the same site when the CTD casts show the lake stratified on both dates.
  • Hypolimnetic mass. The mean of the HYP and BOT samples multiplied by the volume below the thermocline (from the hypsograph, using the mean thermocline depth of the two casts).
  • Flux. The change in that mass divided by the time between samples and by the sediment area below the thermocline.
  • These are lower bounds. The lake mixes partially between monthly samples, so some released mass is lost upward. Positive intervals are summarised below.

2. Hypolimnetic oxygen depletion (from the buoys).

  • Which periods are used. Each buoy-recorded stratified period (at least 7 days) that starts with bottom DO above 2 g/m³.
  • Volumetric rate. A regression of daily bottom DO against time, up to the point where DO first falls below 1 g/m³.
  • Areal hypolimnetic oxygen demand (AHOD). The volumetric rate multiplied by the mean hypolimnion thickness below the median thermocline depth of the period.
  • What it measures. AHOD is a measure of total sediment and hypolimnetic oxygen demand. It is directly comparable with chamber sediment oxygen demand (SOD).

Oxygen depletion results.

  • Across 42 well-fitted stratified periods (r² ≥ 0.5), bottom DO fell at a median 0.28 g/m³/d.
  • The median AHOD is 1.5 g O₂/m²/d (interquartile range 1.0–2.0).
  • The depletion rate rises with bottom temperature, with θ ≈ 1.056 per °C. That is about 32 % faster at 20 °C than at 15 °C. This is one mechanism by which warmer summers strengthen internal loading.
  • The AHOD values sit within the chamber SOD range of Burger et al. (2007), 0.3–4.0 g O₂/m²/d in the light chambers. They are towards the upper part of it, which is expected, because AHOD also includes oxygen demand in the water column below the thermocline.

Converting oxygen demand to nutrient release. If the oxygen demand were all due to the mineralisation of Redfield organic matter (O₂:N:P = 138:16:1 by mole), the median AHOD would correspond to:

  • about 76 mg N/m²/d
  • about 10.5 mg P/m²/d

This is an upper-bound-type estimate for N. For P it is an estimate of mineralisation only. It excludes P released when iron oxides are reduced, which can be larger, and it excludes the P bound by alum.

How the estimates compare.

  • NH₄.
    • The accumulation estimates (median about 25 mg N/m²/d, up to about 80) are at the low end of the chamber rates (33–224 mg N/m²/d in the secondary phase).
    • The oxygen-demand-based estimate (about 78 mg N/m²/d) sits within them.
    • Together they put summer NH₄ release in Rotorua at roughly 25–100 mg N/m²/d, so the model’s zone-1 value (about 93 mg N/m²/d) is plausible.
    • The two deeper zones (34 and 9 mg N/m²/d) look low.
  • DRP.
    • Accumulation (2004–2024) gives about 2–5 mg P/m²/d (maximum about 8 mg P/m²/d, in 2019/20 and 2020/21).
    • That is at the lower end of the 2003–2004 chamber range (2–86 mg P/m²/d in the light chambers), which was measured before alum dosing.
    • The model’s fsed_frp (0.8–2 mg P/m²/d) is at or below the lowest observed value. It may be too low unless the model supplies P to the sediment by another route (settling and mineralisation, or the alum-bound pool).
  • Oxygen. AHOD (interquartile range about 1.0–2.1 g O₂/m²/d) is consistent with the chamber SOD. It is above fsed_oxy (0.75–0.96 g O₂/m²/d), but the difference is mostly accounted for by water-column demand below the thermocline.

Scaling up to an annual internal load.

  • Sediment area below the median summer thermocline: about 38 km².
  • Release rates:
    • DRP: 2.4–5.3 mg/m²/d
    • NH₄: 26–76 mg N/m²/d

What the scaling suggests.

  • P. Each summer’s stratified periods release in the order of 3–15 t of DRP in most years, against an external TP load of about 28–60 t/y. It was lowest in the barely stratified 2009/10 (about 1–2 t) and highest in 2021/22 (about 7–16 t).
  • N. NH₄-N release is in the order of 30–230 t, against an external TN load of 380–815 t/y.

Limits of these totals.

  • They cover only the stratified bottom layer in December–February.
  • They exclude release during mixed periods. Burger et al. (2007) found SRP release largely independent of DO.
  • They exclude release from the shallower sediments, which occupy the larger area.

So these are conservative. Earlier whole-lake budgets put internal loading at a substantial share of the total P load. These monitoring-based rates are best treated as the floor of the range the model’s sediment fluxes should reproduce.

6 Sudden changes and events

6.1 Event definitions

All events are detected automatically from continuous daily series (functions in R/wq_synthesis_helpers.R; targets met_events and buoy_events).

6.2 Examples

What the examples show.

  • Air heatwaves in Rotorua typically peak 2–6 °C above the climatology and last 1–3 weeks. Since 2016 they have occurred in winter as well as summer. August 2022 was the most intense (“Severe”), with daily means near 15 °C.
  • Lake surface heatwaves follow air heatwaves combined with light winds.
    • The December 2017 event warmed the surface to about 23 °C while the bottom stayed near 16 °C.
    • It set up a 41-day stratified period, and bottom DO fell from about 10 g/m³ to below 2 g/m³ in about four weeks.
  • Mixing ends the anoxia. Mixing in late December, then the storm of early January 2018 (149 mm in 3 days, daily mean wind up to 14 m/s), reoxygenated the bottom water. A hot spell then re-stratified the lake: a second lake heatwave in late January brought the surface to 24.7 °C, and anoxia returned within two weeks.
  • Storms are mostly 2–4-day events, and the modelled total inflow responds within a day.
    • Late January 2023: inflow peaked at 83 m³/s and stayed above about 25 m³/s for three weeks.
    • Cyclone Gabrielle: more rain (229 mm in 3 days), and the windiest of the large storms (daily mean wind about 15 m/s), but a smaller modelled inflow peak (about 34 m³/s).
    • The July 2025 sequence: three storms in five weeks, ending with the second-largest 3-day total on record (285 mm) and an inflow peak of about 60 m³/s.

6.3 Detected change points

The change points group into a few episodes.

  1. 1996–2002. TN, chl-a and organic N increase. This is the late-1990s deterioration that led to the 2000s blooms.
  2. 2007–2010. TP, DRP and particulate P fall, and TN, chl-a and organic N fall about a year later. This follows the start of dosing at Utuhina in 2006 and Puarenga in 2010.
  3. 2011. A step up in NOx at both sites, with a similar step in several streams in April 2011. It may be partly analytical.
  4. 2019–2021. A step up in TP (Jun–Nov 2020) and DRP (2019–2021), reversed in late 2022. This aligns with the Puarenga dosing pause.
  5. 2015 and 2021–2023. Steps up in TN and NH₄ (bottom NH₄ from Jan 2015; INT NH₄ Apr 2021; TN Jul 2021 and Feb 2023).

6.5 Storm events and the lake response

Large storms.

  • The largest storms in the continuous record are:
    • July 2004
    • late July 2025 (285 mm in 3 days)
    • May 2010
    • January 2011
    • Cyclone Gabrielle (13–14 February 2023)
    • April 2017 (two events)
    • March 2016
  • Flows. Modelled total inflow peaked at 97 m³/s on 29 April 2018 and 83 m³/s on 28 January 2023, the highest since the 1960s.

Lake response.

  • No consistent spike. Monthly sampling seldom catches storm effects. Across 40 large storms, the first sample after a storm was not significantly different in TP, DRP, TN or turbidity. Chl-a was slightly higher (median +1.1 mg/m³, p ≈ 0.05).
  • Lasting effects. These come through the annual loads and through mixing:
    • The wet years 2011–2013, 2017–2018 and 2023–2024 carried 40–60 t/y of TP and 570–815 t/y of TN (alum_storm_central), well above the PC10 targets.
    • The cool, windy and wet summer of 2022/23 (the Gabrielle summer) had little stratification, well-oxygenated bottom water and the lowest bottom DRP of the decade.
    • The large N load of 2023–2024 is the most likely source of the higher TN and NOx in 2023–2026.

6.6 Case studies

2009/10 – 2010/11: the last high-internal-load period before full dosing.

  • Long stratification (51 days in summer 2008/09, 46 days in 2010/11) brought repeated anoxia.
  • Bottom DRP reached 116 mg/m³ and NH₄ 732 mg/m³ at Site 5 in 2010.
  • Full dosing on both streams from 2011 (88–100 t Al/y) was followed by the lowest TP (13–17 mg/m³) and TLI on record in 2012–2013, despite the wet, high-load years 2011–2013.

2014/15 and 2015/16: hot summers under dosing.

  • 2015 had the 30-day anoxic period, and bottom DRP and NH₄ returned to 56–78 and 490–670 mg/m³.
  • Surface TP stayed near 20 mg/m³.
  • Internal release persisted under dosing, but was not mixed into the epilimnion in large amounts.

2019–2022: dosing pause, heat and drought.

  • Dosing pause. Puarenga dosing dropped to 6.5 t Al in 2018/19 and to zero in 2019/20, and resumed during 2020/21.
  • Weather.
    • 2019 was very dry (794 mm), and 2019 and 2020 were warm and sunny.
    • 2020/21 and 2021/22 had the lowest external TP loads since the 1990s (28–29 t/y).
    • Summer 2021/22 brought an air heatwave (21 days, Dec 2021 – Jan 2022), a strong lake heatwave and 70 days of stratification.
  • Lake response.
    • Bottom DRP reached 108–118 mg/m³ and TP 157–162 mg/m³.
    • Surface DRP and TP rose to the highest values since 2010.
  • Reading. With external loads at a minimum, this points to an internal source. The sediment P was no longer being capped by Puarenga alum, and was then released under strong, long stratification. See Section 6.7 for the sequence and why the model misses it.
  • Recovery. TP fell back in 2022/23, after the resumption of dosing (64 t Al from Puarenga in 2022/23) and in a cool, wet, well-mixed summer.

2023 – 2026: wet years, high N loads, rising TN and chl-a.

  • 2023 was the wettest year in the ERA5 record (2478 mm), followed by an above-average 2024.
  • The 2023 TN load (814 t/y) was the highest since 1990, and 2024 (706 t/y) was also well above the target.
  • Lake TN, NH₄ and NOx rose, and in 2026 chl-a rose and Secchi depth fell.
  • TP has stayed near 19–21 mg/m³ under steady dosing (50–96 t Al/y).

6.7 The 2019–2022 phosphorus excursion

Lake TP doubled between 2017–2020 and 2021–2022, from about 15 to 27–32 mg/m³ (annual means of the integrated samples). This was the largest change since full dosing began. The evidence below points to internal loading that built up while Puarenga dosing was paused, amplified by the hot, long-stratified summer of 2021/22.

What the data show.

  • The model’s alum input and the dosing record agree. Puarenga dosing fell to 6.5 t Al in 2018/19, stopped from September 2018 to November 2020, and then resumed (Figure 21). Utuhina dosing continued unchanged.
  • Summer bottom-water P built up year on year during the pause. January–March bottom DRP rose from about 1 mg/m³ (2018) to 8.5 (2019), 22 (2020) and 34 (2021 and 2022). Bottom TP rose from 22 to 37, 43 and about 70 mg/m³ over the same years (Figure 22).
  • The build-up mixed into the surface. Each autumn (April–June), when the lake mixes, the bottom-water P spread through the water column. Surface TP reached 39 mg/m³ in early 2021 and 42 mg/m³ in autumn 2022. In winter it fell back to 14–23 mg/m³, but from a higher base each year.
  • It was not driven by external load. External TP loads in 2020/21 and 2021/22 were the lowest since the 1990s (28–29 t/y, against 40–60 t/y in 2017–2018), and inflow volumes were low.
  • Stratification amplified it, but only at the end. Summers 2018/19 to 2020/21 were not unusually stratified. Summer 2021/22 had the longest stratified period on record (70 days), an air heatwave, a strong lake heatwave and the warmest December–February air temperature in the record (19.4 °C). It produced the peak bottom-water DRP and TP (108–118 and 157–162 mg/m³).
  • Recovery lagged the restart of dosing by about two summers. Bottom-water P stayed high in summers 2020/21 and 2021/22, after dosing resumed in November 2020. It fell sharply only from late 2022. That followed heavier dosing (96 t Al in 2022/23) and the cool, wet, well-mixed 2023.
  • It is not a laboratory artefact. The finer reporting from 2021 (Section 3.6) affects low DRP values. It cannot produce a doubling of TP, or bottom TP of 70–160 mg/m³.

Interpretation.

  • How Puarenga dosing caps the sediment. The Puarenga is dosed at about 33 mol Al per mol P, far more than is needed to bind the stream’s P. The excess aluminium enters the lake as floc and keeps a P-binding layer on the lake bed, where it limits the release of P during low-oxygen periods.
  • What happened when it stopped. With no fresh floc, the binding capacity aged and was buried over one to two years. Each summer’s anoxic release was then less suppressed than the summer before.
  • The 2021/22 peak. The very long stratification of summer 2021/22 coincided with weakened sediment binding, and produced the peak.
  • The recovery. It needed the binding layer to rebuild, which took about two summers of renewed dosing.
  • What this implies. The lake’s phosphorus state depends on the continuity of dosing more than on the size of the external load. A break in dosing shows up as rising internal loading within one to two summers.

Why the model does not reproduce it.

  • The input is correct. The calibration inflows contain the pause: Puarenga alum is zero from September 2018 to October 2020. Puarenga inflow FRP rises about fourfold over the pause, because the in-stream reaction no longer removes it.
  • The modelled TP bump is early and short. It comes from that extra stream FRP and the wet 2017–2018 years, peaking in 2018–2019. It is then flushed out, so all 24 members simulate TP falling by 5–8 mg/m³ from 2017–2020 to 2021–2022, where the lake rose by about 14 (?@fig-pause-model).
  • The sediment alum pool reacts too fast. It loses about 60% of its capacity in the first year of the pause and recovers within a year of dosing restarting. In the lake, the binding loss and the recovery each took about two years.
  • Gross sediment P release is fixed. It is a constant flux in the model (aed_sed_const2d) and changes by only 5–8% across the pause. The model has no store of sediment P whose release grows when the alum binding is lost.

The misfit is therefore structural, and calibrating parameters cannot remove it: the one-stage calibration with this response as a target left it unchanged (z about −8; calibration/wq_targets/README.md). The model needs two additions:

  • a sediment P pool that accumulates and is released when the binding capacity falls;
  • an alum binding capacity in the sediment that decays over one to two years.

The analysis is in calibration/wq_targets/pause/.

7 Observational drivers

7.1 Meteorology

Agreement between sources. The buoy and aerodrome records agree with the bias-corrected ERA5 on year-to-year variability. The bias correction was fitted to them, so this is expected.

What the continuous ERA5-BC record shows.

  • Warming. About +1 °C from the late 1970s to the 2020s. 2019–2025 are among the warmest years, and 2022 is the warmest (14.6 °C).
  • Heatwaves. A step-like increase in air-heatwave days after 2016, to mostly 25–55 days per hydrological year. Before then it was typically 5–20 days, with peaks in 1990, 1998–1999 and 2011.
  • Rain. Large swings: dry 2015 and 2019, against very wet 2010–2011, 2017 and 2022–2023.
  • ENSO. La Niña years (2011–2012, 2021–2023) tend to be warm and humid, sometimes wet.
  • Calm spells. Summer wind has no clear trend. The calmest summers (2014/15 and 2017/18) match the longest stratification and the largest surface–bottom temperature differences.

7.2 Alum dosing

History of dosing.

  • Utuhina. About 13–38 t Al/y since mid-2006.
  • Puarenga. About 40–75 t Al/y from 2011 to 2018. This was cut to 6.5 t in 2018/19 and to zero in 2019/20, then resumed part-way through 2020/21 (20 t), peaking at 64 t Al in 2022/23 (the year after the TP excursion).
  • Total. Dosing peaked at 101 t Al/y in 2011/12 and was 96 t Al/y in 2022/23.

Compliance monitoring (2025–2026).

  • Dosing lowers Puarenga DRP by about 70 %, from a median of 0.031 to 0.009 mg/L.
  • TP is unchanged (the P is bound in particulate form).
  • Al rises by about 0.8 mg/L, and pH drops by about 0.8 units.
  • April 2026 storm sample: TP was 0.21–0.22 mg/L both upstream and downstream, and upstream Al was 3.3 mg/L.

7.3 River water quality

Stream nutrients. The streams are groundwater-dominated, with high nitrate and mostly geological phosphorus.

  • Nitrogen.
    • Median TN is 0.8–1.6 g/m³ at most streams and 2.6 g/m³ at the geothermally influenced Waiohewa. NOx makes up 85–95 % of the TN, except at the Waiohewa, where about half is NH₄.
    • Over the full record, NOx and TN rose by about 0.5–1 %/yr at Awahou, Hamurana, Waingaehe, Waiteti and Utuhina. This is the slow arrival of old, nitrate-rich groundwater.
    • Over the last decade most N trends are flat, but Hamurana TN and NOx are still rising (+1.3–1.5 %/yr).
    • Upper Waiteti (Oturoa Rd) has fallen sharply since 2018, which suggests a land-use change.
  • DRP.
    • DRP has decreased significantly at almost every site over both periods.
    • The largest fall is at the Utuhina (−3 to −6 %/yr), which is dosed with alum.
    • Ōhau outlet DRP is at detection limits.
  • TP.
    • TP shows no full-record trend at most sites.
    • It decreases by 3–6 %/yr over 2016–2026 at almost every site. This is part of the synchronous 2011–2019 rise and fall (Figure 26, Section 3.6), so treat it with caution.
  • Concentration and flow. These slopes are calculated against modelled daily flow, so they are indicative only.
    • TSS rises steeply with flow at every stream (b = 0.4–1.8).
    • TP rises moderately at the Waiteti, Ngongotaha, Hamurana and Puarenga (b = 0.2–0.5): particulate P is mobilised in storms.
    • DRP is diluted at most streams (b = −0.2 to −0.6).
    • TN and NOx change little with flow (b mostly 0.1–0.4), as expected for groundwater-dominated nitrogen.
    • This is why storm sampling, and the stormflow particulate correction in the PC10 loads, matter for TP but much less for TN.

8 Modelled inflows: PC10 2026 loads

Patterns in the modelled loads. These are modelled loads (McBride 2026), so they show how flow, concentration and the interpolation between monthly samples interact, rather than independent observations.

  • Year-to-year variability is driven by flow.
    • TN ranges from 380 to 815 t/y, and TP from 28 to 60 t/y.
    • The highest-load years are the wettest: 2011–2012, 2018 and 2023–2024.
    • In those years the stormflow particulate correction adds 5–13 t P/y.
  • Against the targets. In 2020–2022, TN loads were below the 435 t N/y target and TP was below 37 t/y. 2023 and 2024 were well above both targets. In 2025 TP was below target but TN (467 t/y) was above it.
  • Recent sequence.
    • Low loads in 2020–2022 were followed by the highest N load on record in 2023.
    • That is the opposite of the lake TP response in the same years. Lake TP rose in the low-load years and fell in the high-load year, which again points to internal processes and alum, not external load, as the control on year-to-year lake TP.
  • Where the P comes from.
    • The groundwater streams Hamurana and Awahou supply most of the dissolved P, which is high-FRP and not dosed.
    • The Puarenga and Utuhina supply most of the particulate P.
    • In the alum scenarios, dosing removes about 2–3 t FRP/y in recent years, and up to 4 t in 2011 (alum against noalum).
  • Lake response to N. TN loads were high in 2023–2024, and lake TN rose by about 15 % between 2021 and 2024. Lake TN also responded to the high-load years 2011–2013, but only after a delay.

8.1 How drivers relate to the lake, year by year

Reading the correlations.

  • Level correlations mostly reflect the shared trend over time. Alum dose is the strongest correlate of lake TP, DRP, TN and TLI (ρ −0.6 to −0.84). Air temperature also correlates negatively with lake TP, but only because both changed over the same decades.
  • Year-to-year (differenced) correlations are more informative about process:
    • Warmer summers come with lower minimum bottom DO (ρ ≈ −0.58) and higher bottom NH₄ maxima (ρ ≈ +0.57).
    • Wetter, higher-flow years come with lower bottom DRP maxima (ρ ≈ −0.6).
    • Higher-load years come with lower lake TP than the year before. External load does not drive year-to-year lake TP, while flushing and mixing in wet years appear to suppress internal loading.
    • Windier years come with clearer water.
  • Overall. Climate controls the strength of internal loading from year to year, and alum and the external loads set the baseline.
  • Caveats: about 30 years of data, many tests, autocorrelation, and analytical changes in the record. Treat these as hypotheses for the model to test, not as attribution.

9 Targets for the model

The patterns described above can be turned into a set of observation-based targets: features of the lake record that a credible model of Lake Rotorua should reproduce. Each target has five parts:

  • Measure: a precisely defined calculation (a “signature”).
  • Observed value: the value of that measure in the monitoring record.
  • Tolerance: usually the year-to-year variability of the signature in the observations. Within one tolerance, the model is as close as the lake is to itself from one year to the next.
  • Priority:
    1. what the model must get right for management questions: physics, oxygen and the phosphorus response to alum;
    2. the internal-loading process, nitrogen and seasonal timing;
    3. secondary patterns.
  • Scoring: the same code computes every signature from model output, and the run is scored by z = (model − observed) / tolerance.

The code is in R/wq_target_metrics.R:

  • wq_target_signatures() computes the signatures.
  • model_target_inputs() builds the inputs from GLM-AED profile output, sampling the model on the observed dates and layers.
  • score_wq_targets() and score_wq_summary() score a run.

The observed values are in the wq_obs_targets target.

Why these priorities.

  • Priority 1 contains what the model’s main uses depend on: alum-dosing scenarios, the PC10 load targets and climate projections.
    • Physics and oxygen: stratification frequency and spell length, thermocline depth, summer surface temperature, and the frequency and depth of bottom anoxia. These control internal loading.
    • The size of the alum response: TP before and after dosing and their ratio.
    • The response to the 2019–2021 Puarenga dosing pause: about +14 mg/m³ of TP in 2021–2022. It is the only “experiment” in the record, and the strongest test that the alum and sediment modules respond for the right reasons.
    • The resulting chl-a and TLI under alum.
  • Priority 2 covers how the internal load is generated:
    • the bottom-water maxima of NH₄ and DRP;
    • the January–February bottom excess;
    • the rates of oxygen depletion and nutrient accumulation;
    • the timing of the surface peaks: TP in March, NH₄ in April, NOx in July;
    • TN levels.
    A model can match annual TP for the wrong reasons, for example with the internal load replaced by too much external P. These targets guard against that.
  • Priority 3 covers features that are useful checks but are weakly constrained or affected by the data problems in Section 3.6: the winter NOx peak, TN:TP, Secchi depth, and the recent trends in chl-a and TP.

How to score.

  • Per target: |z| ≤ 1 is good (within the lake’s own interannual variability), 1–2 acceptable, and > 2 poor.
  • Overall: a priority-weighted mean of z², with weights 3, 2 and 1.
  • Not scored: targets the run cannot cover (e.g. 2023–2026 for a run that ends in 2022, or Secchi depth, which the model does not output) are left unscored rather than penalised.
  • Calibration and validation: the signatures can be used directly as calibration terms, in the same way as the CSPS terms in calibration/csps_metrics.R. The dosing-pause and recent-period targets are better kept for validation.

9.1 Example: the current calibrated ensemble

What the example shows. This is the current one-stage calibrated ensemble: 24 members, run to June 2022, with the historical dosing and the PC10-era inflows. The priority-weighted mean z² of the members is 9–18 (median about 12.5).

  • Physics and oxygen are good. Every member is within one tolerance for:
    • summer surface temperature;
    • the share of stratified days and the longest stratified spell;
    • the share of anoxic summer days, AHOD and the rate of bottom DO decline.
    The thermocline is about 3 m too deep (14 against 11 m), and bottom water is slightly less anoxic at its annual minimum (z about +1.3).
  • The alum response is roughly right in size, but the levels are low.
    • Pre-alum TP is close (39 against 41 mg/m³).
    • Alum-era TP is too low (13 against 17 mg/m³), so the modelled alum effect is somewhat too strong (ratio 0.34 against 0.42).
    • Chl-a and TN under alum are low by about 1.5–2 tolerances.
  • The dosing-pause response is wrong in sign. Every member simulates TP falling by about 6 mg/m³ from 2017–2020 to 2021–2022, where the lake rose by about 14 mg/m³ (z about −8). For the same reason the recent TP trend has the wrong sign, but the model’s window ends in 2022, so that comparison is partial. This matches the step 3 finding that the model “stays low in 2020–22”: in the model the dosing pause does not release enough P.
  • Internal loading is too weak.
    • Bottom NH₄ maxima are about 9 times too low (34 against 311 mg/m³).
    • The January–February bottom NH₄ excess is almost absent (4 against 120 mg/m³).
    • Hypolimnetic NH₄ and DRP accumulation are 15–50 times below even the observed lower bounds.
    • Yet oxygen depletion is right. So the model consumes oxygen at the bed at the right rate but releases far too little NH₄ and DRP into the bottom water, or mixes it away too quickly.
    • The bottom DRP maximum and the January–February DRP excess score “good” only because their tolerances are very wide (DRP release is very variable from year to year). They constrain the model weakly; the accumulation flux is the more informative DRP target.
  • The seasonal timing is wrong.
    • Surface TP peaks in August in the model, against March in the lake.
    • NH₄ peaks in July–August, against April.
    • NOx (August against July) is about right.
    • In the lake, the autumn peaks come from the summer internal load being mixed upwards. In the model, the winter peaks come from external loads in the wet season.

What this means for the next calibration. The weakest targets, in order, are:

  1. The dosing-pause response.
  2. Bottom-water NH₄ and DRP accumulation, with the related autumn timing of TP and NH₄.
  3. The alum-era TP, TN and chl-a levels.

All three point to the sediment-release terms (fsed_amm, fsed_frp and their oxygen and temperature dependence) and to the alum–sediment interaction, rather than to physics. Adding the priority 1 and 2 signatures to the calibration objective would put direct weight on them.

10 Synthesis: state of the lake

Past (1990s – 2006).

  • Lake Rotorua moved from mesotrophic-to-eutrophic conditions to a strongly eutrophic state.
    • TLI3 reached 5.1 in 2003–2004.
    • There were chl-a blooms of 20–30 mg/m³ annual mean.
    • Bottom-water anoxia was frequent, with large internal P and N loads.
  • External loads were high (TN 450–640 t/y, TP 30–60 t/y) and N loads were rising with nitrate-rich groundwater.

Recovery (2006 – about 2015).

  • Alum dosing of the Utuhina (2006) and Puarenga (2010) brought a rapid, step-like improvement, and full dosing of both streams from 2011 reinforced it.
    • TP and DRP halved.
    • Chl-a and organic N followed within about a year.
    • Clarity improved.
    • The 3-year mean TLI stayed close to the target of 4.2 (4.15–4.28) from about 2013 to 2021.
  • This happened even though external loads in 2011–2013 were among the highest of the period. This is strong evidence that the in-lake P binding by alum, not external load reduction, drove the improvement.

Present (2016 – 2026).

  • The lake is now in a “held” but slowly deteriorating state.
    • TLI3 is 4.3–4.4 (above target).
    • TP is 17–21 mg/m³, except in the 2021–2022 excursion.
    • TN and chl-a are rising.
    • Summer stratification and anoxia still occur in hot, calm summers.
  • Phosphorus. The 2019–2022 episode shows how sensitive the lake is to the continuity of dosing. When Puarenga dosing paused, a hot summer with long stratification was enough to return surface DRP and TP to pre-2011 levels within two years.
  • Nitrogen. The lake is drifting upwards after the large 2023–2024 loads. Stream nitrate is still rising in the groundwater-fed streams (e.g. Hamurana).

Risks and trends to watch.

  1. Warming. About +1 °C over 50 years, with air-heatwave days roughly tripling since 2016, favours longer stratification and more internal loading.
  2. More frequent extreme wet years. 2011, 2017–2018 and 2023 each bring 700–800+ t N/y and 50–60 t P/y. Wet years mix the lake in the short term but add N and particulate P that is recycled later.
  3. Dependence on alum. The lake’s P state relies on continuous dosing. Any reduction is likely to show up within 1–2 summers.
  4. Data continuity. Changes in lab and reporting resolution blur trends in low-level DRP and NH₄ and in river TP. The buoy pH and CTD chlorophyll sensors need checking.

What this means for modelling.

  • The calibration period should include the dosing pause (2019–2021) and the 2021/22 heat and stratification. Together they are the strongest test of the alum and sediment modules.
  • Lake TN after 2022 is a test of the PC10 2026 N loads.
  • Trends in DRP and river TP across the 2015 and 2020 lab boundaries should not be calibrated to until the lab history is confirmed.

**The lake today.** Lake Rotorua is a eutrophic, polymictic lake.

- The 3-year mean TLI3 for 2024–2026 is **`r sprintf("%.2f", tli_last$tli3_3yr)`** (hydrological year `r tli_last$hy`).
  - This is above the target of 4.2.
  - It is well below the 2004–2006 peak of `r sprintf("%.2f", tli_max$tli3_3yr)`.
  - It is higher than the 2014–2020 low of `r sprintf("%.2f", tli_min$tli3_3yr)`.
- Integrated surface-water means for 2023–2026: TP ≈ `r round(tp_2326)` mg/m³, TN ≈ `r round(tn_2326)` mg/m³, chl-a ≈ `r round(ch_2326, 1)` mg/m³.

**Three phases in the record.**

1. **Early-2000s eutrophic peak.**
   - TP ≈ `r round(tp_0106)`, TN ≈ `r round(tn_0106)`, chl-a ≈ `r round(ch_0106)` mg/m³ (2001–2006).
   - Frequent bottom-water anoxia, with summer bottom NH₄ and DRP above 400 and 50 mg/m³.
2. **Alum-era recovery, 2007 to about 2012.**
   - TP fell by about half, to ≈ `r round(tp_1220)` mg/m³ (2012–2020).
   - TN fell to ≈ `r round(tn_1220)` mg/m³ and chl-a roughly halved.
   - The step changes in TP, DRP and particulate P are detected in 2007–2010. They follow Utuhina dosing (from June 2006) and Puarenga dosing (from January 2010).
3. **A partial reversal since about 2016.** Over the last 10 years TP, TN and chl-a show significant increasing trends at both sites, and Secchi depth is decreasing at Site 5. Two things stand out:
   - **The 2020–2022 TP excursion.** Mean TP was ≈ `r round(tp_2122)` mg/m³ in 2021–2022. Bottom TP reached 160 mg/m³ and DRP 110–120 mg/m³ in summer 2021/22. Three things coincided:
     - a near-complete pause in Puarenga dosing (`r paste(format(pua_pause_main$start, "%b %Y"), "to", format(pua_pause_main$end, "%b %Y"))`);
     - the strongest lake heatwave and the longest buoy-recorded stratification (70 days, Nov 2021 – Jan 2022);
     - two low-load years (external TP ≈ 28–29 t/y).

     TP returned to about 19 mg/m³ once dosing resumed and the very wet 2023 brought a well-mixed year.
   - **Nitrogen is rising.** TN rose from about 300 to about 360 mg/m³ after 2021. This follows the high-flow 2023 and 2024 hydrological years, when modelled external TN loads were 814 and 706 t/y against the target of 435 t/y. In-lake NH₄ and NOx are also higher than in the 2010s.

**Drivers.**

- **Alum** is the strongest single correlate of lake TP and TLI (ρ ≈ −0.8). It is confounded with the time trend, but the 2019–2021 dosing pause acts as a natural experiment that supports a causal link.
- **Internal loading.** Year-to-year changes in summer air temperature track bottom-water oxygen and NH₄ accumulation. Hot, calm summers produce long stratification, anoxia and internal P and N release: 2008/09, 2010/11, 2014/15, 2017/18, 2021/22 and 2023/24.
- **Internal release rates.** The bottom layer below the thermocline (typically 9–12 m deep in summer) is only about 5 m thick on average. During stratified periods:
  - it loses oxygen at about 1.5 g O₂/m²/d (the depletion rate rises about 5 % per °C);
  - it gains NH₄ at about 25–80 mg N/m²/d and DRP at about 2–5 mg P/m²/d (lower bounds).

  These rates suggest roughly 3–15 t of DRP and 30–230 t of NH₄-N are released each summer from the deep sediments alone (@sec-internal).
- **External loads.** Wet years raise external loads (2011–2013, 2017–2018, 2023–2024) but tend to flush and mix the lake, so they reduce peak bottom DRP.
- **Warming.** The bias-corrected ERA5 record warms by about 1 °C over 1977–2025. Air-heatwave days have increased from typically 5–20 per year before 2016 to mostly 25–55 per year since then.

**Data-quality caveats.** These affect the interpretation of trends.

- The lake DRP, NH₄, NOx and TP records show changes in reporting resolution: zeros and negatives in 2015–2020, non-integer TP in 2015–2018, and finer decimals from 2021. These point to laboratory or method changes, so the "increasing DRP" trend over the last 10 years is not reliable.
- A synchronous TP rise and fall (2011–2019) across all streams, including spring-fed ones, is more likely analytical than catchment-driven.
- See @sec-dq.

**Notes on the data.**

- **Hydrological years.** Lake summaries use July–June hydrological years, named by the year they end. For example, 2022 is July 2021 to June 2022, and its summer is December 2021 to February 2022.
- **INT samples** are depth-integrated over the epilimnion (0 m to roughly 6–14 m).
- **BOT samples** are taken about 1 m above the sediment, at 18–22 m.
- **Trend tests** are seasonal Mann–Kendall tests on monthly medians with Sen slopes. They are not corrected for serial correlation, so p-values are optimistic.
- **Change points** are binary segmentation of deseasonalised monthly anomalies, with a conservative penalty.

**Known caveats.**

- **Site codes.** The alkalinity file uses EL805168 for Site 5, against EL802148 here.

**Phosphorus.**

- **Before alum (1990s–2006).** Integrated TP was mostly 30–48 mg/m³. A high 1993/94 (≈ 80 mg/m³) reflects very few samples in that year.
- **Fall with dosing.** TP fell from about 40 mg/m³ in 2006 to 15–20 mg/m³ from 2011/12.
- **Composition.** DRP dropped from 5–15 mg/m³ to mostly ≤ 2 mg/m³ in the surface water. Most of the remaining TP is particulate or organic (TP − DRP), which also halved, from about 30 to 15 mg/m³.
- **2021–2022.** TP was clearly elevated (27–33 mg/m³), with DRP rising to 4–8 mg/m³. It then fell back to 18–21 mg/m³ in 2023–2026.

**Nitrogen.**

- **Peak.** TN was highest in 2000–2007 (≈ 450–575 mg/m³ at Site 2).
- **Step down.** It stepped down around 2009/10 (change point Aug 2009 at Site 2, Feb 2008 at Site 5) to about 300 mg/m³ in 2011–2021. The drop is almost all organic N (TKN − NH₄), which follows the fall in phytoplankton biomass.
- **Since 2022.** TN has risen to 340–365 mg/m³.
- **Inorganic N** is more variable:
  - NH₄ peaks in years of strong internal loading.
  - NOx-N was close to detection before 2011 and much higher afterwards: years such as 2012–2013, 2017–2018 and 2023–2024 have annual means of 40–140 mg/m³.
  - The NOx step in 2011 appears at both lake sites and, in part, in several streams. It may be partly analytical (@sec-dq), but high NOx after winter mixing is a real feature of the lake.

**Chlorophyll-a and clarity.**

- **Chl-a** peaked in 2001–2007 (20–30 mg/m³ annual means) and fell to 8–13 mg/m³ after 2012.
- **Secchi** depth improved from about 2.5 to about 3 m.
- **Last decade.** Both have edged back towards poorer conditions: chl-a 12–16 mg/m³ and Secchi 2.4–2.5 m in 2026.

**The seasonal cycle.**

- **Late summer and autumn (Feb–May).** After stratification breaks down, nutrients released from the sediment are mixed into the surface water. TP, DRP and NH₄ peak at this time.
- **Winter and spring (Jul–Oct).** NOx-N peaks, as the NH₄ is nitrified and the lake stays mixed.

**How the cycle has changed.**

- **TP and DRP.** The late-summer and autumn TP peak (March–April) is about half of its pre-alum size (about 27 against 56 mg/m³), and similar to 2006–2016. The large autumn DRP peak of the pre-alum era (about 20 mg/m³ in March) has disappeared.
- **Ammonium.** The autumn NH₄ peak is now the largest of the three periods (median about 50 mg/m³ in April).
- **Nitrate.** The winter NOx peak (July–October, about 60–100 mg/m³) is much larger than before.
- **Nitrogen overall.** Together these suggest that N released from the sediment in summer is now mixed and nitrified in winter, rather than taken up by phytoplankton.
- **Chl-a.** It no longer shows the large summer–autumn maxima of the early 2000s, and in the last decade the seasonal range is only about 8–14 mg/m³.

## Internal loading {#sec-internal}

Lake Rotorua is polymictic. It does not stratify through the whole summer like a deep monomictic lake. Instead it stratifies for days to weeks at a time between about October and March, and wind events mix it in between. Each stratified period isolates a thin bottom layer, roughly 3–8 m thick, over the deep sediments. That layer loses oxygen quickly and gains NH₄ and DRP released from the sediment. The next mixing event carries those nutrients into the surface water. This section uses the temperature profiles (buoys and CTD), the bottom-water chemistry and the lake hypsography to describe this cycle and to estimate the release rates.

**When the lake stratifies.**

- Stratification starts to develop in October and November.
- It is most frequent and strongest from December to February. On about half of all summer days the surface is at least 1 °C warmer than the bottom, and Schmidt stability on calm, hot days exceeds 100–300 J/m².
- It collapses in March as the lake cools. From April to August the lake is fully mixed.

### Estimating release rates (fluxes)

Two independent estimates are possible from the monitoring data. Both are compared with the sediment-chamber fluxes measured in the lake by Burger et al. (2007) and with the values in the model's `aed.nml`.

**1. Hypolimnetic accumulation (NH₄, DRP, TP).**

- **Which samples are used.** Pairs of consecutive monthly samples at the same site when the CTD casts show the lake stratified on both dates.
- **Hypolimnetic mass.** The mean of the HYP and BOT samples multiplied by the volume below the thermocline (from the hypsograph, using the mean thermocline depth of the two casts).
- **Flux.** The change in that mass divided by the time between samples and by the sediment area below the thermocline.
- **These are lower bounds.** The lake mixes partially between monthly samples, so some released mass is lost upward. Positive intervals are summarised below.

```{r}
gN_per_gO2 <- 16 * 14.007 / (138 * 31.998); gP_per_gO2 <- 30.974 / (138 * 31.998)
pos <- function(v) median(hflux$flux[hflux$variable == v & hflux$flux > 0])
```

**Oxygen depletion results.**

- Across `r nrow(hod_ok)` well-fitted stratified periods (r² ≥ 0.5), bottom DO fell at a median `r sprintf("%.2f", rate_med)` g/m³/d.
- The median AHOD is **`r sprintf("%.1f", ahod_med)` g O₂/m²/d** (interquartile range `r sprintf("%.1f–%.1f", ahod_iqr[1], ahod_iqr[2])`).
- The depletion rate rises with bottom temperature, with θ ≈ `r sprintf("%.3f", theta)` per °C. That is about `r sprintf("%.0f", 100 * (theta^5 - 1))` % faster at 20 °C than at 15 °C. This is one mechanism by which warmer summers strengthen internal loading.
- The AHOD values sit within the chamber SOD range of Burger et al. (2007), 0.3–4.0 g O₂/m²/d in the light chambers. They are towards the upper part of it, which is expected, because AHOD also includes oxygen demand in the water column below the thermocline.

**Converting oxygen demand to nutrient release.** If the oxygen demand were all due to the mineralisation of Redfield organic matter (O₂:N:P = 138:16:1 by mole), the median AHOD would correspond to:

- about **`r sprintf("%.0f", ahod_med * 1000 * gN_per_gO2)` mg N/m²/d**
- about **`r sprintf("%.1f", ahod_med * 1000 * gP_per_gO2)` mg P/m²/d**

This is an upper-bound-type estimate for N. For P it is an estimate of mineralisation only. It excludes P released when iron oxides are reduced, which can be larger, and it excludes the P bound by alum.

**Scaling up to an annual internal load.**

- Sediment area below the median summer thermocline: about `r sprintf("%.0f", a_sed / 1e6)` km².
- Release rates:
  - DRP: `r sprintf("%.1f–%.1f", q_drp[1], q_drp[2])` mg/m²/d
  - NH₄: `r sprintf("%.0f–%.0f", f_nh4[1], f_nh4[2])` mg N/m²/d

**What the scaling suggests.**

- **P.** Each summer's stratified periods release in the order of 3–15 t of DRP in most years, against an external TP load of about 28–60 t/y. It was lowest in the barely stratified 2009/10 (about 1–2 t) and highest in 2021/22 (about 7–16 t).
- **N.** NH₄-N release is in the order of 30–230 t, against an external TN load of 380–815 t/y.

**Limits of these totals.**

- They cover only the stratified bottom layer in December–February.
- They exclude release during mixed periods. Burger et al. (2007) found SRP release largely independent of DO.
- They exclude release from the shallower sediments, which occupy the larger area.

So these are conservative. Earlier whole-lake budgets put internal loading at a substantial share of the total P load. These monitoring-based rates are best treated as the floor of the range the model's sediment fluxes should reproduce.

**Large storms.**

- The largest storms in the continuous record are:
  - July 2004
  - late July 2025 (285 mm in 3 days)
  - May 2010
  - January 2011
  - Cyclone Gabrielle (13–14 February 2023)
  - April 2017 (two events)
  - March 2016
- **Flows.** Modelled total inflow peaked at 97 m³/s on 29 April 2018 and 83 m³/s on 28 January 2023, the highest since the 1960s.

**Lake response.**

- **No consistent spike.** Monthly sampling seldom catches storm effects. Across 40 large storms, the first sample after a storm was not significantly different in TP, DRP, TN or turbidity. Chl-a was slightly higher (median +1.1 mg/m³, p ≈ 0.05).
- **Lasting effects.** These come through the annual loads and through mixing:
  - The wet years 2011–2013, 2017–2018 and 2023–2024 carried 40–60 t/y of TP and 570–815 t/y of TN (alum_storm_central), well above the PC10 targets.
  - The cool, windy and wet summer of 2022/23 (the Gabrielle summer) had little stratification, well-oxygenated bottom water and the lowest bottom DRP of the decade.
  - The large N load of 2023–2024 is the most likely source of the higher TN and NOx in 2023–2026.

### Case studies

**2009/10 – 2010/11: the last high-internal-load period before full dosing.**

- Long stratification (51 days in summer 2008/09, 46 days in 2010/11) brought repeated anoxia.
- Bottom DRP reached 116 mg/m³ and NH₄ 732 mg/m³ at Site 5 in 2010.
- Full dosing on both streams from 2011 (88–100 t Al/y) was followed by the lowest TP (13–17 mg/m³) and TLI on record in 2012–2013, despite the wet, high-load years 2011–2013.

**2014/15 and 2015/16: hot summers under dosing.**

- 2015 had the 30-day anoxic period, and bottom DRP and NH₄ returned to 56–78 and 490–670 mg/m³.
- Surface TP stayed near 20 mg/m³.
- Internal release persisted under dosing, but was not mixed into the epilimnion in large amounts.

**2019–2022: dosing pause, heat and drought.**

- **Dosing pause.** Puarenga dosing dropped to 6.5 t Al in 2018/19 and to zero in 2019/20, and resumed during 2020/21.
- **Weather.**
  - 2019 was very dry (794 mm), and 2019 and 2020 were warm and sunny.
  - 2020/21 and 2021/22 had the lowest external TP loads since the 1990s (28–29 t/y).
  - Summer 2021/22 brought an air heatwave (21 days, Dec 2021 – Jan 2022), a strong lake heatwave and 70 days of stratification.
- **Lake response.**
  - Bottom DRP reached 108–118 mg/m³ and TP 157–162 mg/m³.
  - Surface DRP and TP rose to the highest values since 2010.
- **Reading.** With external loads at a minimum, this points to an internal source. The sediment P was no longer being capped by Puarenga alum, and was then released under strong, long stratification. See @sec-pause for the sequence and why the model misses it.
- **Recovery.** TP fell back in 2022/23, after the resumption of dosing (64 t Al from Puarenga in 2022/23) and in a cool, wet, well-mixed summer.

**2023 – 2026: wet years, high N loads, rising TN and chl-a.**

- 2023 was the wettest year in the ERA5 record (2478 mm), followed by an above-average 2024.
- The 2023 TN load (814 t/y) was the highest since 1990, and 2024 (706 t/y) was also well above the target.
- Lake TN, NH₄ and NOx rose, and in 2026 chl-a rose and Secchi depth fell.
- TP has stayed near 19–21 mg/m³ under steady dosing (50–96 t Al/y).

### The 2019–2022 phosphorus excursion {#sec-pause}

**What the data show.**

- **The model's alum input and the dosing record agree.** Puarenga dosing fell to 6.5 t Al in 2018/19, stopped from September 2018 to November 2020, and then resumed (@fig-pause-sequence). Utuhina dosing continued unchanged.
- **Summer bottom-water P built up year on year during the pause.** January–March bottom DRP rose from about 1 mg/m³ (2018) to 8.5 (2019), 22 (2020) and 34 (2021 and 2022). Bottom TP rose from 22 to 37, 43 and about 70 mg/m³ over the same years (@fig-pause-summers).
- **The build-up mixed into the surface.** Each autumn (April–June), when the lake mixes, the bottom-water P spread through the water column. Surface TP reached 39 mg/m³ in early 2021 and 42 mg/m³ in autumn 2022. In winter it fell back to 14–23 mg/m³, but from a higher base each year.
- **It was not driven by external load.** External TP loads in 2020/21 and 2021/22 were the lowest since the 1990s (28–29 t/y, against 40–60 t/y in 2017–2018), and inflow volumes were low.
- **Stratification amplified it, but only at the end.** Summers 2018/19 to 2020/21 were not unusually stratified. Summer 2021/22 had the longest stratified period on record (70 days), an air heatwave, a strong lake heatwave and the warmest December–February air temperature in the record (19.4 °C). It produced the peak bottom-water DRP and TP (108–118 and 157–162 mg/m³).
- **Recovery lagged the restart of dosing by about two summers.** Bottom-water P stayed high in summers 2020/21 and 2021/22, after dosing resumed in November 2020. It fell sharply only from late 2022. That followed heavier dosing (96 t Al in 2022/23) and the cool, wet, well-mixed 2023.
- **It is not a laboratory artefact.** The finer reporting from 2021 (@sec-dq) affects low DRP values. It cannot produce a doubling of TP, or bottom TP of 70–160 mg/m³.

**Interpretation.**

- **How Puarenga dosing caps the sediment.** The Puarenga is dosed at about 33 mol Al per mol P, far more than is needed to bind the stream's P. The excess aluminium enters the lake as floc and keeps a P-binding layer on the lake bed, where it limits the release of P during low-oxygen periods.
- **What happened when it stopped.** With no fresh floc, the binding capacity aged and was buried over one to two years. Each summer's anoxic release was then less suppressed than the summer before.
- **The 2021/22 peak.** The very long stratification of summer 2021/22 coincided with weakened sediment binding, and produced the peak.
- **The recovery.** It needed the binding layer to rebuild, which took about two summers of renewed dosing.
- **What this implies.** The lake's phosphorus state depends on the continuity of dosing more than on the size of the external load. A break in dosing shows up as rising internal loading within one to two summers.

**Why the model does not reproduce it.**

- **The input is correct.** The calibration inflows contain the pause: Puarenga alum is zero from September 2018 to October 2020. Puarenga inflow FRP rises about fourfold over the pause, because the in-stream reaction no longer removes it.
- **The modelled TP bump is early and short.** It comes from that extra stream FRP and the wet 2017–2018 years, peaking in 2018–2019. It is then flushed out, so all 24 members simulate TP falling by 5–8 mg/m³ from 2017–2020 to 2021–2022, where the lake rose by about 14 (@fig-pause-model).
- **The sediment alum pool reacts too fast.** It loses about 60% of its capacity in the first year of the pause and recovers within a year of dosing restarting. In the lake, the binding loss and the recovery each took about two years.
- **Gross sediment P release is fixed.** It is a constant flux in the model (`aed_sed_const2d`) and changes by only 5–8% across the pause. The model has no store of sediment P whose release grows when the alum binding is lost.

The misfit is therefore structural, and calibrating parameters cannot remove it: the one-stage calibration with this response as a target left it unchanged (z about −8; `calibration/wq_targets/README.md`). The model needs two additions:

- a sediment P pool that accumulates and is released when the binding capacity falls;
- an alum binding capacity in the sediment that decays over one to two years.

The analysis is in `calibration/wq_targets/pause/`.

**Stream nutrients.** The streams are groundwater-dominated, with high nitrate and mostly geological phosphorus.

## Targets for the model {#sec-model-targets}

The patterns described above can be turned into a set of **observation-based targets**: features of the lake record that a credible model of Lake Rotorua should reproduce. Each target has five parts:

- **Measure:** a precisely defined calculation (a "signature").
- **Observed value:** the value of that measure in the monitoring record.
- **Tolerance:** usually the year-to-year variability of the signature in the observations. Within one tolerance, the model is as close as the lake is to itself from one year to the next.
- **Priority:**
  1. what the model must get right for management questions: physics, oxygen and the phosphorus response to alum;
  2. the internal-loading process, nitrogen and seasonal timing;
  3. secondary patterns.
- **Scoring:** the same code computes every signature from model output, and the run is scored by z = (model − observed) / tolerance.

The code is in `R/wq_target_metrics.R`:

- `wq_target_signatures()` computes the signatures.
- `model_target_inputs()` builds the inputs from GLM-AED profile output, sampling the model on the observed dates and layers.
- `score_wq_targets()` and `score_wq_summary()` score a run.

The observed values are in the `wq_obs_targets` target.

```{r}
#| label: tbl-model-targets
#| tbl-cap: "Observation-based targets for the Lake Rotorua model, by priority. Tolerance: ± one tolerance; for log-scaled targets, a factor (×/÷); for timing, ± months. n is the number of years (or events) behind the value. Hypolimnetic accumulation fluxes are lower bounds: a model at or above them meets the target."
obs_t <- tr("wq_obs_targets")
mabb <- function(x) month.abb[pmin(pmax(round(ifelse(is.finite(x), x, 1)), 1), 12)]
fmt_v <- function(x) ifelse(abs(x) >= 100, sprintf("%.0f", x), ifelse(abs(x) >= 10, sprintf("%.1f", x), sprintf("%.2f", x)))
obs_t |>
  mutate(Tolerance = case_when(
           metric %in% log_scale_metrics ~ sprintf("×/÷ %.1f", exp(tol)),
           grepl("^month_max", metric) ~ "± 1 month",
           TRUE ~ paste("±", fmt_v(tol))),
         Observed = ifelse(grepl("^month_max", metric), mabb(value), fmt_v(value))) |>
  arrange(priority, theme) |>
  transmute(Priority = priority, Theme = theme, Target = label, Measure = measure, Period = period,
            Observed, Tolerance, n = n) |>
  kable()
```

**Why these priorities.**

- **Priority 1** contains what the model's main uses depend on: alum-dosing scenarios, the PC10 load targets and climate projections.
  - **Physics and oxygen:** stratification frequency and spell length, thermocline depth, summer surface temperature, and the frequency and depth of bottom anoxia. These control internal loading.
  - **The size of the alum response:** TP before and after dosing and their ratio.
  - **The response to the 2019–2021 Puarenga dosing pause:** about +14 mg/m³ of TP in 2021–2022. It is the only "experiment" in the record, and the strongest test that the alum and sediment modules respond for the right reasons.
  - **The resulting chl-a and TLI under alum.**
- **Priority 2** covers how the internal load is generated:
  - the bottom-water maxima of NH₄ and DRP;
  - the January–February bottom excess;
  - the rates of oxygen depletion and nutrient accumulation;
  - the timing of the surface peaks: TP in March, NH₄ in April, NOx in July;
  - TN levels.

  A model can match annual TP for the wrong reasons, for example with the internal load replaced by too much external P. These targets guard against that.
- **Priority 3** covers features that are useful checks but are weakly constrained or affected by the data problems in @sec-dq: the winter NOx peak, TN:TP, Secchi depth, and the recent trends in chl-a and TP.

**How to score.**

- **Per target:** |z| ≤ 1 is good (within the lake's own interannual variability), 1–2 acceptable, and > 2 poor.
- **Overall:** a priority-weighted mean of z², with weights 3, 2 and 1.
- **Not scored:** targets the run cannot cover (e.g. 2023–2026 for a run that ends in 2022, or Secchi depth, which the model does not output) are left unscored rather than penalised.
- **Calibration and validation:** the signatures can be used directly as calibration terms, in the same way as the CSPS terms in `calibration/csps_metrics.R`. The dosing-pause and recent-period targets are better kept for validation.

**What the example shows.** This is the current one-stage calibrated ensemble: 24 members, run to June 2022, with the historical dosing and the PC10-era inflows. The priority-weighted mean z² of the members is 9–18 (median about 12.5).

- **Physics and oxygen are good.** Every member is within one tolerance for:
  - summer surface temperature;
  - the share of stratified days and the longest stratified spell;
  - the share of anoxic summer days, AHOD and the rate of bottom DO decline.

  The thermocline is about 3 m too deep (14 against 11 m), and bottom water is slightly less anoxic at its annual minimum (z about +1.3).
- **The alum response is roughly right in size, but the levels are low.**
  - Pre-alum TP is close (39 against 41 mg/m³).
  - Alum-era TP is too low (13 against 17 mg/m³), so the modelled alum effect is somewhat too strong (ratio 0.34 against 0.42).
  - Chl-a and TN under alum are low by about 1.5–2 tolerances.
- **The dosing-pause response is wrong in sign.** Every member simulates TP falling by about 6 mg/m³ from 2017–2020 to 2021–2022, where the lake rose by about 14 mg/m³ (z about −8). For the same reason the recent TP trend has the wrong sign, but the model's window ends in 2022, so that comparison is partial. This matches the step 3 finding that the model "stays low in 2020–22": in the model the dosing pause does not release enough P.
- **Internal loading is too weak.**
  - Bottom NH₄ maxima are about 9 times too low (34 against 311 mg/m³).
  - The January–February bottom NH₄ excess is almost absent (4 against 120 mg/m³).
  - Hypolimnetic NH₄ and DRP accumulation are 15–50 times below even the observed lower bounds.
  - Yet oxygen depletion is right. So the model consumes oxygen at the bed at the right rate but releases far too little NH₄ and DRP into the bottom water, or mixes it away too quickly.
  - The bottom DRP maximum and the January–February DRP excess score "good" only because their tolerances are very wide (DRP release is very variable from year to year). They constrain the model weakly; the accumulation flux is the more informative DRP target.
- **The seasonal timing is wrong.**
  - Surface TP peaks in August in the model, against March in the lake.
  - NH₄ peaks in July–August, against April.
  - NOx (August against July) is about right.
  - In the lake, the autumn peaks come from the summer internal load being mixed upwards. In the model, the winter peaks come from external loads in the wet season.

**What this means for the next calibration.** The weakest targets, in order, are:

1. The dosing-pause response.
2. Bottom-water NH₄ and DRP accumulation, with the related autumn timing of TP and NH₄.
3. The alum-era TP, TN and chl-a levels.

All three point to the sediment-release terms (`fsed_amm`, `fsed_frp` and their oxygen and temperature dependence) and to the alum–sediment interaction, rather than to physics. Adding the priority 1 and 2 signatures to the calibration objective would put direct weight on them.



## Synthesis: state of the lake {#sec-synthesis}

**Past (1990s – 2006).**

- Lake Rotorua moved from mesotrophic-to-eutrophic conditions to a strongly eutrophic state.
  - TLI3 reached 5.1 in 2003–2004.
  - There were chl-a blooms of 20–30 mg/m³ annual mean.
  - Bottom-water anoxia was frequent, with large internal P and N loads.
- External loads were high (TN 450–640 t/y, TP 30–60 t/y) and N loads were rising with nitrate-rich groundwater.

**Recovery (2006 – about 2015).**

- Alum dosing of the Utuhina (2006) and Puarenga (2010) brought a rapid, step-like improvement, and full dosing of both streams from 2011 reinforced it.
  - TP and DRP halved.
  - Chl-a and organic N followed within about a year.
  - Clarity improved.
  - The 3-year mean TLI stayed close to the target of 4.2 (4.15–4.28) from about 2013 to 2021.
- This happened even though external loads in 2011–2013 were among the highest of the period. This is strong evidence that the in-lake P binding by alum, not external load reduction, drove the improvement.

**Present (2016 – 2026).**

- The lake is now in a "held" but slowly deteriorating state.
  - TLI3 is 4.3–4.4 (above target).
  - TP is 17–21 mg/m³, except in the 2021–2022 excursion.
  - TN and chl-a are rising.
  - Summer stratification and anoxia still occur in hot, calm summers.
- **Phosphorus.** The 2019–2022 episode shows how sensitive the lake is to the continuity of dosing. When Puarenga dosing paused, a hot summer with long stratification was enough to return surface DRP and TP to pre-2011 levels within two years.
- **Nitrogen.** The lake is drifting upwards after the large 2023–2024 loads. Stream nitrate is still rising in the groundwater-fed streams (e.g. Hamurana).

**Risks and trends to watch.**

1. **Warming.** About +1 °C over 50 years, with air-heatwave days roughly tripling since 2016, favours longer stratification and more internal loading.
2. **More frequent extreme wet years.** 2011, 2017–2018 and 2023 each bring 700–800+ t N/y and 50–60 t P/y. Wet years mix the lake in the short term but add N and particulate P that is recycled later.
3. **Dependence on alum.** The lake's P state relies on continuous dosing. Any reduction is likely to show up within 1–2 summers.
4. **Data continuity.** Changes in lab and reporting resolution blur trends in low-level DRP and NH₄ and in river TP. The buoy pH and CTD chlorophyll sensors need checking.

**What this means for modelling.**

- The calibration period should include the dosing pause (2019–2021) and the 2021/22 heat and stratification. Together they are the strongest test of the alum and sediment modules.
- Lake TN after 2022 is a test of the PC10 2026 N loads.
- Trends in DRP and river TP across the 2015 and 2020 lab boundaries should not be calibrated to until the lab history is confirmed.
