Benthic Nutrient Fluxes in Lake Rotorua (Burger et al. 2007)

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Reference: Burger, D.F., Hamilton, D.P., Pilditch, C.A. & Gibbs, M.M. (2007). Benthic nutrient fluxes in a eutrophic, polymictic lake. Hydrobiologia 584: 13–25. doi:10.1007/s10750-007-0582-0

This page summarises the paper and tabulates its chamber data so the fluxes can be used to characterise, and later constrain, the sediment-release terms in the Lake Rotorua GLM-AED model. Data are in data/burger2007_chamber_fluxes.csv and data/burger2007_bottom_water.csv, transcribed from Tables 1 and 2 of the paper.

Summary

Aim and approach

The authors measured sediment release of soluble reactive phosphorus (SRP) and ammonium (NH4), and sediment oxygen demand (SOD), using in situ benthic chambers at three sites in Lake Rotorua. They then compared internal (sediment) loads with the external catchment load.

  • Lake: 79.8 km², mean depth 10.5 m, polymictic, eutrophic. Annual mean TP 0.055 mg L⁻¹, TN 0.814 mg L⁻¹. Catchment 425 km² (48% agriculture, 23% plantation forestry). Wastewater was discharged to the lake until 1991 (about 35 t TP and 150 t TN per year).
  • Sites: Site 1 (7 m, well mixed and oxygenated), Site 2 (14 m, periodically stratified) and Site 3 (20 m, periodically stratified and anoxic).
  • Timing: four deployments: 10 Feb 2003, 13 Aug 2003, 4/10 Nov 2003 and 27 Jan 2004.
  • Chambers:
    • Acrylic and PVC cylinders of about 0.11 m² and 17–18 L, pushed 80 mm into the sediment by divers.
    • Duplicate light and dark chambers at Sites 1 and 2. Only light chambers at Site 3, where light was below 1% of surface PAR.
    • Pumped for 5 s per minute. Sampled about four times a day for 2 days.
    • Fluxes come from linear regression of concentration against time, corrected for dilution, with control bottles subtracted.
    • The 48 h deployment was long enough to make the chambers anoxic at Sites 2 and 3 in summer, which mimics natural stratification events.

Key findings

  1. SRP release was large and mostly independent of oxygen. Rates were 2.1–85.6 mg P m⁻² d⁻¹, and concentrations rose linearly in the chambers (mean R² = 0.93) regardless of chamber DO. This is above the 7–50 mg m⁻² d⁻¹ typical of other eutrophic lakes. The authors link it to high organic matter deposition and microbial P cycling at the sediment surface, rather than to Fe-P redox release alone.
  2. NH4 release had two phases.
    • A very high initial rate over 0–12 h of up to about 2,200 mg N m⁻² d⁻¹.
    • A lower secondary rate over 12–48 h of up to about 270 mg N m⁻² d⁻¹ (maximum 283 in the dark chambers).
    • The authors suggest the initial burst may partly be a chamber or porewater disturbance artefact, and that the secondary rate better represents natural organic matter degradation under low DO.
  3. Release peaks in summer and in deep water. The pattern held for SRP, NH4 and SOD. August (winter, about 10 °C) rates were the lowest, and Site 3 (20 m) had the highest SRP and NH4 release. Site 3 SRP (mean 44.3 mg m⁻² d⁻¹) was significantly higher than Sites 2 (9.3) and 1 (7.7) in all periods except August. Temperature and the organic matter supply rate (sedimentation) are the proposed drivers.
  4. Light and dark chambers differed little at Site 1. Site 2 dark chambers had higher initial NH4 release and, in Feb 2003, about three times higher SRP release (29.4 vs 9.0 mg m⁻² d⁻¹), so benthic primary production appears to matter little for fluxes.
  5. The chamber rates agree with whole-lake estimates. For the 19-day stratification event starting 1 Feb 2003:
    • SRP release from chambers, interpolated by depth below the thermocline, was 17.4 mg m⁻² d⁻¹. The independent hypolimnetic mass balance gave 13.8.
    • NH4 release from the chambers’ secondary rate was 131 mg m⁻² d⁻¹. The hypolimnion accumulation gave 63.9, so the chambers over-estimate NH4 by about 2× (they miss nitrification and benthic uptake).
  6. Internal load exceeds external load. Mean sediment SRP and NH4 release were at least three times the mean daily external load. At Site 3, SRP release exceeded the TP sedimentation rate. The authors expect high release to continue because of the large legacy sediment nutrient pool, and say only a severe reduction in external load would “break” the depositional cycle.

Caveats stated or implied

  • Only SRP and NH4 were measured. Dissolved organic P and N, porewater release by resuspension (important in the shallows), and water-column denitrification are not included.
  • NO3 fluxes were not calculated because chamber concentrations were below 5% of NH4 and often below detection.
  • The sample size is small (n = 2 chambers per site and period, 4 in Jan 2004 at Site 3), and Site 3 has no dark chambers.
  • The text says TP sedimentation was 0.5–2.7 g m⁻² d⁻¹. That is inconsistent with the 13–29 mg m⁻² d⁻¹ shown in Fig. 6, so it is probably a units slip in the paper.

Characterising sediment fluxes for Lake Rotorua

Recommended flux ranges

  • SRP: 2–14 mg P m⁻² d⁻¹ in the shallow and mid-depth regions (Sites 1–2) and about 6–86 mg P m⁻² d⁻¹ at 20 m. Winter (about 10 °C) is the low end and summer stratification the high end. About 17 mg m⁻² d⁻¹ is a reasonable mean beneath the thermocline in summer, based on the paper’s own validation.
  • NH4: use the secondary (12–48 h) rate, 33–283 mg N m⁻² d⁻¹, which the authors argue is more representative. The initial burst (up to 2,213) should not be used directly. Even the secondary rate over-estimates the hypolimnion mass balance by about 2×, so treat it as an upper bound.
  • SOD: 0.3–4.0 g O2 m⁻² d⁻¹, with lake-wide means of about 0.9 / 1.5 / 1.9 g m⁻² d⁻¹ at 7 / 14 / 20 m.

Conversion to model units

AED expects fluxes in mmol m⁻² d⁻¹. The conversions use 30.974 g mol⁻¹ for P, 14.007 for N and 31.998 for O2.

Code 
site_means_mmol <- fl |>
  filter(chamber == "light") |>
  group_by(Site = site, `Depth (m)` = depth_m) |>
  summarise(
    `Fsed_frp (mmol P/m²/d)` = mean(srp_mmol),
    `Fsed_amm (mmol N/m²/d, NH4-2)` = mean(nh4_2_mmol),
    `Fsed_oxy (mmol O2/m²/d, as demand)` = mean(sod_mmol),
    .groups = "drop"
  )
kable(site_means_mmol, digits = 2)

Comparison with the current model settings

The current aed.nml sets fsed_amm = 6.654, 2.408, 0.616, fsed_frp = 0.0602, 0.0657, 0.0254 and fsed_oxy = -30.1, -30.1, -23.33 mmol m⁻² d⁻¹. I have not checked which sediment zone each entry maps to.

  • fsed_oxy is in the same range as the chamber SOD (about 27 / 47 / 60 mmol O2 m⁻² d⁻¹ at 7 / 14 / 20 m). Chamber SOD is somewhat larger at depth.
  • fsed_amm is within the chamber range for the first zone (6.65 against 6.6 mmol N m⁻² d⁻¹ at Site 1), but the other two zones (2.4 and 0.6) are well below the secondary chamber rates (about 4.4 at 14 m and 9.7 at 20 m).
  • fsed_frp is much lower than the chamber SRP rates (0.25 / 0.30 / 1.43 mmol P m⁻² d⁻¹ at 7 / 14 / 20 m). The model values are about 4–50 times smaller.

Whether that gap is a calibration artefact or a real model–observation mismatch depends on how the model treats other P sources. These include phytoplankton and POP settling and mineralisation, and the alum-bound P pool in the alum module. In AED the sediment fluxes also scale with temperature (theta_sed_frp, theta_sed_amm) and with a DO half-saturation (ksed_frp, ksed_amm). The paper’s finding that SRP release was largely independent of DO argues against making the P flux strongly oxygen-dependent.

Indicative temperature dependence

Aug-to-Jan ratios of light-chamber release over the bottom-water temperature range give an indicative Arrhenius θ. The estimate is confounded with seasonal changes in organic matter supply, so use it only as a sense check.

Code 
fl |>
  filter(chamber == "light", period %in% c("Aug 2003", "Jan 2004")) |>
  left_join(bw |> select(site, date, temp_C), by = c("site", "date")) |>
  select(site, period, temp_C, srp_mg_m2_d, nh4_phase2_mg_m2_d) |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = period,
              values_from = c(temp_C, srp_mg_m2_d, nh4_phase2_mg_m2_d)) |>
  mutate(
    dT = `temp_C_Jan 2004` - `temp_C_Aug 2003`,
    `theta SRP` = (`srp_mg_m2_d_Jan 2004` / `srp_mg_m2_d_Aug 2003`)^(1 / dT),
    `theta NH4-2` = (`nh4_phase2_mg_m2_d_Jan 2004` / `nh4_phase2_mg_m2_d_Aug 2003`)^(1 / dT)
  ) |>
  select(Site = site, `dT (°C)` = dT, `theta SRP`, `theta NH4-2`) |>
  kable(digits = 2)

The implied θ is about 1.12–1.17 for SRP and 1.08–1.12 for NH4. The SRP values are higher than the model setting (theta_sed_frp = 1.06), while NH4 is close to theta_sed_amm = 1.08. The authors suggest temperature and organic matter supply act together on seasonal release, so the SRP θ is likely inflated by the seasonal sedimentation cycle.

Implications for the model

  1. Treat sediment release as a major term. Internal loading is at least 3× the external load according to the paper, so calibration should not assume external load dominates.
  2. Spatial structure matters. Release at 20 m is 5–6× that at 7–14 m for SRP, so the zone-specific Fsed_* values should increase with depth.
  3. Season and temperature should be represented. Winter release was about 10× lower than summer at Site 3.
  4. A weak oxygen dependence for P is supported by the chambers. This is a different result from the usual anoxic-release assumption, but it applies to 48 h incubations only.
  5. Use as calibration targets. The 2003–2004 period overlaps the buoy and chemistry record, so the 17 mg P m⁻² d⁻¹ (hypolimnetic, Feb 2003) and 64 mg N m⁻² d⁻¹ mass-balance values are useful independent checks on simulated hypolimnetic accumulation during stratification.

# Characterising sediment fluxes for Lake Rotorua

## Recommended flux ranges

-   **SRP:** 2–14 mg P m⁻² d⁻¹ in the shallow and mid-depth regions (Sites 1–2) and about 6–86 mg P m⁻² d⁻¹ at 20 m. Winter (about 10 °C) is the low end and summer stratification the high end. About 17 mg m⁻² d⁻¹ is a reasonable mean beneath the thermocline in summer, based on the paper's own validation.
-   **NH~4~:** use the secondary (12–48 h) rate, 33–283 mg N m⁻² d⁻¹, which the authors argue is more representative. The initial burst (up to 2,213) should not be used directly. Even the secondary rate over-estimates the hypolimnion mass balance by about 2×, so treat it as an upper bound.
-   **SOD:** 0.3–4.0 g O~2~ m⁻² d⁻¹, with lake-wide means of about 0.9 / 1.5 / 1.9 g m⁻² d⁻¹ at 7 / 14 / 20 m.

## Conversion to model units

AED expects fluxes in mmol m⁻² d⁻¹. The conversions use 30.974 g mol⁻¹ for P, 14.007 for N and 31.998 for O~2~.

```{r}
#| label: tbl-aed-units
site_means_mmol <- fl |>
  filter(chamber == "light") |>
  group_by(Site = site, `Depth (m)` = depth_m) |>
  summarise(
    `Fsed_frp (mmol P/m²/d)` = mean(srp_mmol),
    `Fsed_amm (mmol N/m²/d, NH4-2)` = mean(nh4_2_mmol),
    `Fsed_oxy (mmol O2/m²/d, as demand)` = mean(sod_mmol),
    .groups = "drop"
  )
kable(site_means_mmol, digits = 2)
```

## Comparison with the current model settings

The current [`aed.nml`](../LID11133_rotorua/glm_aed/aed/aed.nml) sets `fsed_amm = 6.654, 2.408, 0.616`, `fsed_frp = 0.0602, 0.0657, 0.0254` and `fsed_oxy = -30.1, -30.1, -23.33` mmol m⁻² d⁻¹. I have not checked which sediment zone each entry maps to.

-   **`fsed_oxy`** is in the same range as the chamber SOD (about 27 / 47 / 60 mmol O~2~ m⁻² d⁻¹ at 7 / 14 / 20 m). Chamber SOD is somewhat larger at depth.
-   **`fsed_amm`** is within the chamber range for the first zone (6.65 against 6.6 mmol N m⁻² d⁻¹ at Site 1), but the other two zones (2.4 and 0.6) are well below the secondary chamber rates (about 4.4 at 14 m and 9.7 at 20 m).
-   **`fsed_frp`** is much lower than the chamber SRP rates (0.25 / 0.30 / 1.43 mmol P m⁻² d⁻¹ at 7 / 14 / 20 m). The model values are about 4–50 times smaller.

Whether that gap is a calibration artefact or a real model–observation mismatch depends on how the model treats other P sources. These include phytoplankton and POP settling and mineralisation, and the alum-bound P pool in the [alum module](aed-alum-module.qmd). In AED the sediment fluxes also scale with temperature (`theta_sed_frp`, `theta_sed_amm`) and with a DO half-saturation (`ksed_frp`, `ksed_amm`). The paper's finding that SRP release was largely independent of DO argues against making the P flux strongly oxygen-dependent.

## Indicative temperature dependence

Aug-to-Jan ratios of light-chamber release over the bottom-water temperature range give an indicative Arrhenius θ. The estimate is confounded with seasonal changes in organic matter supply, so use it only as a sense check.

```{r}
#| label: tbl-theta
fl |>
  filter(chamber == "light", period %in% c("Aug 2003", "Jan 2004")) |>
  left_join(bw |> select(site, date, temp_C), by = c("site", "date")) |>
  select(site, period, temp_C, srp_mg_m2_d, nh4_phase2_mg_m2_d) |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = period,
              values_from = c(temp_C, srp_mg_m2_d, nh4_phase2_mg_m2_d)) |>
  mutate(
    dT = `temp_C_Jan 2004` - `temp_C_Aug 2003`,
    `theta SRP` = (`srp_mg_m2_d_Jan 2004` / `srp_mg_m2_d_Aug 2003`)^(1 / dT),
    `theta NH4-2` = (`nh4_phase2_mg_m2_d_Jan 2004` / `nh4_phase2_mg_m2_d_Aug 2003`)^(1 / dT)
  ) |>
  select(Site = site, `dT (°C)` = dT, `theta SRP`, `theta NH4-2`) |>
  kable(digits = 2)
```

The implied θ is about 1.12–1.17 for SRP and 1.08–1.12 for NH~4~. The SRP values are higher than the model setting (`theta_sed_frp = 1.06`), while NH~4~ is close to `theta_sed_amm = 1.08`. The authors suggest temperature and organic matter supply act together on seasonal release, so the SRP θ is likely inflated by the seasonal sedimentation cycle.

# Implications for the model

1.  **Treat sediment release as a major term.** Internal loading is at least 3× the external load according to the paper, so calibration should not assume external load dominates.
2.  **Spatial structure matters.** Release at 20 m is 5–6× that at 7–14 m for SRP, so the zone-specific `Fsed_*` values should increase with depth.
3.  **Season and temperature should be represented.** Winter release was about 10× lower than summer at Site 3.
4.  **A weak oxygen dependence for P** is supported by the chambers. This is a different result from the usual anoxic-release assumption, but it applies to 48 h incubations only.
5.  **Use as calibration targets.** The 2003–2004 period overlaps the buoy and chemistry record, so the 17 mg P m⁻² d⁻¹ (hypolimnetic, Feb 2003) and 64 mg N m⁻² d⁻¹ mass-balance values are useful independent checks on simulated hypolimnetic accumulation during stratification.
