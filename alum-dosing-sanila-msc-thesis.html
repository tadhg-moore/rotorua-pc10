Alum Dosing in Lake Rotorua

Alum Dosing in Lake Rotorua: Thesis Summary and Model Parameterisation Notes

Source: Sanila, S. (2026). Evaluation of Aluminium–Phosphorus Binding and pH Responses to Alum Dosing of Three New Zealand Lakes. MSc thesis, University of Waikato.

1. Thesis Overview

This thesis evaluates the effectiveness of alum (aluminium sulphate, Al₂(SO₄)₃) as a tool for reducing dissolved reactive phosphorus (DRP) across five New Zealand waters: Lake Rotorua, the Utuhina and Puarenga Streams, Lake Ōkaro, and Lake Moawhitu. The study was motivated by a recognised gap in site-specific, controlled evidence for Al:P binding behaviour across the Rotorua catchment lakes, despite many years of operational dosing.

The methodology centred on standardised 3-hour jar tests, with acid titrations used to quantify buffering capacity at each site. For Lake Rotorua specifically, video-based settling measurements and stereomicroscopy were used to characterise the physical properties of alum floc — providing the most detailed floc data available for this lake.

Across all sites, alum reduced DRP, but the magnitude of removal and pH response varied considerably. Al:P molar ratios ranged from 0.7:1 to 15.1:1 depending on site chemistry, and pH behaviour was governed primarily by alkalinity rather than dose rate. Lake Rotorua and the Puarenga Stream had the lowest alkalinity (~19 mg CaCO₃ L⁻¹) and showed the weakest pH recovery after dosing. Lake Moawhitu, with the highest alkalinity (~67 mg CaCO₃ L⁻¹), recovered most strongly.

2. Relevance to &aed_alum Model Parameters

The following sections draw directly on thesis findings to inform parameterisation of the &aed_alum module for Lake Rotorua. Parameters are addressed in thematic groups.

2.1 Binding Stoichiometry and Efficiency (K_P, k_w)

The jar-test Al:P molar ratio for Lake Rotorua was 0.7:1 (mass ratio 0.6:1), based on a dose of 1.0 mg Al L⁻¹ and a measured DRP removal of 1.60 ± 0.008 mg L⁻¹ from a pre-treatment spiked concentration of 1.62 mg L⁻¹. This near 1:1 molar performance is consistent with the lake’s characteristically low dissolved organic carbon (~2 mg L⁻¹), which reduces competition for Al(OH)₃ surface sites and allows a high proportion of added aluminium to remain available for phosphate binding.

It is important to note that this represents a best-case, laboratory-derived estimate. Operational field dosing on the Utuhina and Puarenga inflows has historically required substantially higher effective ratios (~7:1 Al:P; McDowell & Hawke, 2006), reflecting the effects of turbulence, short flocculation contact times, and hydrodynamic losses in the stream environment.

Regarding binding kinetics, DRP removal appeared rapid under jar-test conditions — pH dropped within the first five minutes of dosing, and most removal was complete within the first 20 minutes of the 3-hour experiment. This is qualitatively consistent with a fast water-column binding rate (k_w), though no explicit first-order rate constant was fitted in the thesis. The near-complete removal of 1.62 mg P L⁻¹ (~52 mmol P m⁻³) at a 1 mg Al L⁻¹ dose suggests binding was not substrate-limited at that concentration, which is consistent with a relatively low half-saturation constant (K_P). The default value of 0.3 mmol P m⁻³ is not contradicted by the data, though it cannot be independently confirmed from this study.

2.2 Settling Velocity (w_ala)

The thesis provides the most quantitatively useful constraints for this parameter. Settling velocities were measured for 89 individually tracked flocs under low-turbulence conditions:

Floc size class Typical settling velocity
< 1,000 µm < 40 m day⁻¹
1,500–2,500 µm 50–80 m day⁻¹
> 3,000 µm ~100 m day⁻¹

The fitted linear regression across all flocs was: velocity (m day⁻¹) = 0.0324 × diameter (µm) − 0.94, with R² = 0.69. The median Martin diameter was 1,290 µm and the mean was 1,442 µm. Applying the fitted relationship to Lake Rotorua’s bathymetry, settling through the mean depth (10.8 m) is estimated at 0.2–0.7 days (5–17 hours), and through the maximum depth (45 m) at 0.9–3.0 days under quiescent conditions.

The bulk of the floc population clustered in the 20–60 m day⁻¹ range. A representative central estimate for w_ala of ~0.5 m day⁻¹ (the current default) is physically defensible and consistent with median floc behaviour. A range of 0.3–1.5 m day⁻¹ could be justified depending on whether conservative or mean settling is targeted. These estimates should be treated as upper bounds, as wind-driven mixing in the lake is expected to interrupt settling and prolong suspension.

2.3 Floc Size Distribution (context for settling and resuspension)

Microscopic image analysis of 1,094 fine floc particles revealed:

  • Equivalent diameters ranged from 25 to 240 µm
  • 87% of particles were below 120 µm; only 13% exceeded this
  • Mean equivalent diameter: 77.5 µm; median: 69.3 µm
  • Distribution was right-skewed (skewness = 0.95), dominated by small aggregates

The larger settling aggregates tracked in video (median ~1,290 µm) are composite structures built from these fine sub-units. The thesis notes that alum flocs are inherently fragile and can release stable fine particles under gentle shear — a characteristic relevant to resuspension behaviour under wave action in the lake.

2.4 Resuspension (simResuspension, k_res)

The thesis provides strong qualitative support for enabling resuspension in Lake Rotorua models. Sediment surveys (Tempero & Hamilton, 2016; Tempero, 2019) consistently show that amorphous Al(OH)₃ is concentrated in near-shore areas close to the Utuhina and Puarenga discharge zones, with limited accumulation in the deeper basin sediments. The thesis interprets this pattern as a consequence of fast near-shore settling (consistent with the 0.2–0.7 day estimate to mean depth) combined with subsequent wind-driven resuspension that prevents widespread offshore redistribution.

On this basis, simResuspension = .true. is recommended for Lake Rotorua, particularly in models that resolve spatial differences between near-shore and offshore zones. No quantitative value for k_res is derivable from the thesis; the authors recommend future wave-flume experiments to constrain this parameter.

2.5 pH Efficiency Modifier (ads_use_pH, pH_opt, sigma_pH)

The thesis provides directly relevant pH observations for Lake Rotorua:

  • Pre-treatment pH ranged from 6.9 to 7.9 across sites, with Lake Rotorua at the lower end
  • Following alum addition, pH dropped by up to 2.11 units within 10–20 minutes, then stabilised with minimal recovery (< 0.1 units over 3 hours)
  • Post-treatment pH in jar tests therefore sat persistently in the ~5–6 range
  • Alkalinity was the lowest of all sites at 19.20 mg CaCO₃ L⁻¹, providing very limited buffering against acidification
  • Al(OH)₃ is most stable and P-binding most effective in the pH 6–8 window; efficiency declines below pH 6 and Al³⁺ toxicity becomes a concern below pH 4.5

Since operational dosing is applied via the Utuhina and Puarenga inflows rather than directly to the open lake, pH in the lake body should remain close to ambient (~7–8) rather than dropping to the jar-test minimum. Under open-lake conditions, the pH efficiency modifier is therefore less critical than it would be for a direct-dosing scenario.

If ads_use_pH is activated, a pH_opt of 7.0 would better represent open-lake conditions than the default of 6.0. A sigma_pH of 1.0 is consistent with the observed ±1–2 unit swings recorded during dosing events.

2.6 Dosing Rate (S_ala)

Operational alum dosing has been applied continuously to the Utuhina Stream since 2006 and the Puarenga Stream since 2010, at a target rate of approximately 1 mg Al L⁻¹, adjusted automatically to stream flow. Cumulative aluminium applied reached 1,071 tonnes by end of 2024, at a long-term mean rate of 158.1 kg Al day⁻¹. Peak application occurred during 2011–2012 when more than 2,000 tonnes per year were applied.

Converting the mean operational rate to a whole-lake surface dose: 158.1 kg Al day⁻¹ ÷ 26.98 g mol⁻¹ = ~5,860 mol Al day⁻¹. At the observed 0.7:1 Al:P molar ratio, this binds approximately 8,370 mol P day⁻¹, or ~8.37 × 10⁶ mmol P day⁻¹. Distributed over Lake Rotorua’s surface area (80.6 km² = 80.6 × 10⁶ m²), this gives an indicative whole-lake mean of:

S_ala ≈ 0.10 mmol P m⁻² day⁻¹

Peak dosing years would yield approximately double this value. Note that this is a spatially averaged estimate; in practice, alum enters via discrete inflow points and is not uniformly distributed across the lake surface.

2.7 Ageing and Burial (k_age_w, k_age_s, k_bur)

The thesis does not report explicit rate constants for the loss of binding capacity or burial of settled floc. It does confirm that sediment aluminium persists in near-shore zones over multi-year timescales, implying some long-term retention, but quantitative ageing or burial rates are not derivable from this study. The default values (k_age ~0.0998 day⁻¹, k_bur = 0.01 day⁻¹) cannot be validated or refuted from the available data and should be drawn from the broader literature or calibrated against monitoring data.

4. Key Caveats

The jar-test Al:P ratios are best treated as site-specific indicators of performance under idealised, controlled conditions, not as fixed stoichiometric targets for field dosing. Operational experience consistently shows higher effective Al:P requirements in flowing systems due to turbulence and incomplete flocculation. Settling velocity estimates are similarly best-case figures derived under low-turbulence laboratory conditions; wind-driven mixing in Lake Rotorua will extend effective settling times and promote near-shore deposition over basin-wide distribution. Periodic re-evaluation of jar-test response and alkalinity is recommended, as water chemistry can shift over time and alter both treatment performance and operational risk.
