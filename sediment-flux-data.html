Benthic chamber fluxes (2026 and 2003–04), the 5-zone sediment layout, and how the data constrain GLM-AED

ImportantUnpublished data

The 2026 benthic chamber data are unpublished results from a PhD project, used here with permission: Campbell (pers. comm., 2026). They are shown only as summaries (medians and interquartile ranges by depth). Do not reuse, copy or redistribute them until the results are published.

Summary

  • Benthic chambers were deployed in Lake Rotorua (Campbell, pers. comm., 2026) on 17–20 March 2026 at 10, 15 and 20 m, in light and dark chambers (17 chambers in all). They measured NH4, NOx and PO4 release and sediment oxygen demand (SOD).
  • The fluxes are much lower than in the 2003–04 survey by Burger et al. (2007). At 20 m, PO4 release is about 10× lower (median 4.6 vs 43 mg P/m²/d), NH4 about 6× lower, and SOD about 4× lower.
  • A chamber flux is the release that actually happened at the chamber’s temperature and oxygen. GLM-AED’s fsed_* parameters are maximum rates that the model scales down by temperature and oxygen. The two can only be compared through those scaling terms (below).
  • The model’s 4-zone layout puts the 15 and 20 m chambers in one zone (13–25 m). A 5-zone layout gives each chamber depth its own zone. It is set up as a target (sed_zones) and a calibration base. A new calibration target (ALL_CHAMBER=1) scores each run against the chamber fluxes.

The data

Source: Campbell (pers. comm., 2026), unpublished. The workbook is kept in the project’s restricted OneDrive data folder and read by read_sediment_chamber() into the target sediment_chamber. This page shows only summaries of it.

  • Method. Each rate is the linear-regression slope of chamber concentration over the deployment, times the chamber volume divided by the sediment area. SOD uses only the readings from the start until DO first reached 1 mg/L.
  • Units. Nutrient fluxes are taken as mg N or mg P per m² per day; the workbook header says “mg/m/day”. SOD is in g O2/m²/d.
  • Burger et al. (2007). Chambers at 7, 14 and 20 m in four periods of 2003–04, with the bottom-water DO and temperature on each date (website/data/burger2007_*.csv). Their secondary NH4 rate (12–48 h) is used, which the authors take as the natural release.

Sediment zones on the bathymetry

GLM-AED gives each sediment zone one set of release rates. In AED, each zone’s release also uses the average water conditions over that zone’s depth band. The current model has 4 zones. Its deepest zone covers 13–25 m (29 km²) and so mixes water at 15 m, which stays oxygenated, with the anoxic bottom.

The 2026 sites lie on a west–east line from the western shelf to just west of Mokoia Island, about 3.4 km end to end. On the bathymetry their depths are 9.3, 15.6 and 22.2 m. The 20 m site is therefore a little deeper than its name, but it stays in the deepest zone in both layouts.

The 5-zone layout splits that zone at 17.5 m. This is about where the hypolimnion stays below the thermocline (see the zone release section of the synthesis page), and it gives each chamber depth its own zone.

How a chamber flux relates to AED’s fsed

The chamber method (as in Burger et al. 2007) measures a net flux under the chamber conditions: concentration change × chamber volume / sediment area. AED’s Constant2d model applies a maximum rate fsed, scaled by temperature and by the oxygen in the water above the sediment (mmol/m²/d, O2 in mmol/m³):

Flux AED rate
SOD fsed_oxy × O2/(ksed_oxy + O2) × θ_oxy^(T−20)
NH4 fsed_amm × ksed_amm/(ksed_amm + O2) × θ_amm^(T−20)
FRP fsed_frp × ksed_frp/(ksed_frp + O2) × θ_frp^(T−20)

So a chamber flux equals the model’s actual flux at the chamber’s DO and temperature. It equals fsed only in anoxic water at 20 °C. To get fsed from a chamber flux, divide by the oxygen and temperature factor, which depends on the half-saturation constant ksed. ksed is itself a calibration parameter.

Code 
cm <- summ |> filter(source == "chamber_2026", variable %in% c("NH4", "PO4"))
ks <- 10^seq(0, 2.7, length.out = 60)
K <- tidyr::crossing(cm, ksed = ks) |> rowwise() |>
  mutate(k = list(modifyList(SED_K_SODP, if (variable == "NH4") list(ksed_amm = ksed) else list(ksed_frp = ksed))),
         fsed_mg = median / aed_sed_factor(variable, do_mg_l, temp_c, k)) |> ungroup()
ggplot(K, aes(ksed, fsed_mg, colour = factor(depth_m))) + geom_line(linewidth = 0.8) +
  geom_vline(data = data.frame(variable = c("NH4", "PO4"), v = c(SED_K_SODP$ksed_amm, SED_K_SODP$ksed_frp)), aes(xintercept = v), linetype = 2, colour = "grey40") +
  scale_x_log10() + scale_y_log10() + facet_wrap(~ variable, scales = "free_y", labeller = as_labeller(c(NH4 = "fsed_amm (mg N/m²/d)", PO4 = "fsed_frp (mg P/m²/d)"))) +
  scale_colour_manual(values = c(`10` = "#6da7ec", `15` = "#2a78d6", `20` = "#104281"), name = "depth (m)") +
  labs(x = "ksed (mmol O2/m³)", y = "implied fsed")
  • Oxygen sensitivity. The previous calibration chose a very low ksed_frp (2.6), so P release is about 80× faster without oxygen than at 7 mg/L. Burger et al. found SRP release “largely independent of DO”. In the 2026 data, dark and light chambers release similar PO4 even though DO fell during the deployment. Both point to a weaker oxygen dependence. With ksed_frp near 45 (the default), the chamber medians imply fsed_frp of 15–30 mg P/m²/d rather than 200–400.
  • Earlier starting values. These were taken directly from the measured fluxes as fsed, with an oxygen correction for SOD only. If those measurements were made in oxygenated water, the NH4 and P starting values were too low by this factor.

Comparison with the calibrated model

Passing the previous calibration’s sediment parameters through the AED equations at the 2026 chamber conditions shows three things:

  • Deep zone (15–20 m): NH4 and PO4 release are close to the chambers. They get there through very high anoxic rates that oxygen holds down.
  • 10 m: the calibration had effectively switched release off. The chambers show release comparable to the deeper sites.
  • SOD: the model’s sediment oxygen demand is several times the measured value at every depth. Almost all of the hypolimnetic oxygen depletion was being supplied by the sediment, with little left for water-column respiration.

The calibration fitted the in-lake data with sediment fluxes that are partly inconsistent with direct measurements. This is the motivation for using the chambers as a prior (Section 6).

Using the data in calibration

Where the data enter GLM-AED

GLM-AED’s Constant2d sediment model sets the sediment–water fluxes of oxygen, ammonium and phosphate separately for each sediment zone. Each zone has three parameters per flux:

  • a maximum rate (fsed);
  • an oxygen half-saturation constant (ksed);
  • a temperature coefficient (θ).

Each zone then reacts to the average water conditions over its depth band. The chambers sit at 20, 15 and 10 m. With the five-zone layout (Section 3), each site falls in a different zone, so each zone with data gets its own local measurement. Zones 4 and 5 (0–8 m) have no 2026 data and stay loosely constrained.

Constant2d rates do not change over time. A single parameter set cannot follow the drop in release between Burger et al. (2003–04) and 2026. The chamber data therefore describe the present-day (alum-era) sediment state. The Burger data describe the pre-alum state and suit a run that is scored on that period.

The chambers as a prior

A chamber measures the actual flux at its own oxygen and temperature, not fsed (Section 4). Converting a measured flux to fsed depends on ksed, and ksed is itself uncertain. So a prior placed directly on fsed would carry a hidden assumption about the oxygen dependence. A better prior is one on the flux the model would produce at the chamber’s conditions:

\[ \log F_\text{AED}(f_\text{sed}, k_\text{sed}, \theta \mid \text{DO}_\text{ch}, T_\text{ch}) \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\log \tilde F_\text{ch},\ \sigma^2\right), \qquad \sigma^2 = \sigma_\text{rep}^2 + s^2/n \]

  • \(\tilde F_\text{ch}\) is the chamber median for the zone and variable.
  • \(s^2/n\) is the sampling uncertainty of that median (spread between chambers, n chambers).
  • \(\sigma_\text{rep}\) (about 0.3 in log units, roughly ±35%) covers how well a few chambers represent a whole zone and season.

This is a joint prior on the three parameters. It ties them to the data only through the combination the chambers actually measured, at the oxygen and temperature they measured it. fsed and ksed can still trade off against each other: a high anoxic maximum with strong oxygen suppression, or a lower maximum with weak suppression. Which of these fits is left to the in-lake data. The hypolimnetic build-up of NH4 and DRP during stratification is what separates them. The chambers fix the flux under late-summer, partly oxygenated conditions; the hypolimnion record fixes how it changes as the bottom water goes anoxic.

In practice

  1. Prior range. Each zone with data gets limits set as multiples of the chamber-implied fsed (for example 0.3–3×), rather than the wide limits used before. Zones without data keep wide limits, or borrow a weaker prior from the nearest measured zone.
  2. Prior ensemble. In an ensemble calibration (ES-MDA), the starting members are drawn around the chamber-implied values. The ensemble therefore starts from measured rates rather than from earlier calibrated rates.
  3. Prior term in the objective. Each member’s realized flux at the chamber conditions is computed from its parameters alone (no model run needed) and scored against the chamber median in log space. Because the term is a Gaussian log-likelihood in log space, adding it to the objective is equivalent to the Gaussian prior above. Its weight sets how strongly the prior holds against the lake data.

What the prior adds

  • Splitting oxygen demand. Bulk hypolimnetic oxygen depletion (AHOD) combines sediment and water-column demand. The lake profiles alone cannot separate them. The chamber SOD fixes the sediment share, so the rest has to come from water-column respiration of settling organic matter. This shifts calibration effort toward organic-matter supply and decay, rather than letting sediment demand absorb all of it.
  • Release at intermediate depths. Without a prior, the calibration can switch release off in a zone (as it did at 10 m) and make up for it elsewhere. The chamber prior keeps a measured, non-zero release at each sampled depth.
  • Oxygen dependence. The chambers and Burger et al. both point to weak oxygen control of P release. Together with the flux prior, a prior on ksed_frp centred well above the earlier calibrated value is consistent with both datasets.
  • A check on conflict with the lake data. If the posterior moves far from the chamber prior, the zone fluxes and the in-lake data disagree. That points to another process, such as the organic-matter supply, the mixing or the P sinks, rather than to wrong chamber values.

Caveats

  • Data status. The 2026 chamber data are unpublished (Campbell, pers. comm., 2026). Keep the page to summaries until they are published, and check the published values when they appear.
  • Chamber DO. Each nutrient flux is a regression over the whole deployment, while the DO in the chamber was falling. Using the lake DO as the chamber condition overstates the oxygen. The raw chamber time series (concentration and DO over time) would allow the oxygen dependence (ksed) to be fitted directly.
  • One deployment. The 2026 data cover one late-summer deployment. Seasonal and year-to-year variation (a factor of about 5 between periods in Burger et al.) is not sampled. The 0.3 log tolerance is a judgement and could be widened.
  • No data for 0–3.5 m. Zone 5 has no measurements, and zone 4 (3.5–8 m) has only Burger’s 2003–04 data.

```{r}
                      summarise(Chambers = paste(sprintf("%g m (%s)", depth_m, ifelse(source == "chamber_2026", "2026", "Burger")), collapse = ", ")), by = "zone") |>
  transmute(Zone = zone, `Depth (m)` = paste(top_m, "–", bot_m), `Area (km²)` = round(area_km2, 1), zone_height, Chambers = coalesce(Chambers, "none"))
```

## How a chamber flux relates to AED's `fsed` {#sec-aed}

The chamber method (as in Burger et al. 2007) measures a **net flux under the chamber conditions**: concentration change × chamber volume / sediment area. AED's Constant2d model applies a **maximum rate** `fsed`, scaled by temperature and by the oxygen in the water above the sediment (mmol/m²/d, O2 in mmol/m³):

| Flux | AED rate |
|---|---|
| SOD | `fsed_oxy × O2/(ksed_oxy + O2) × θ_oxy^(T−20)` |
| NH4 | `fsed_amm × ksed_amm/(ksed_amm + O2) × θ_amm^(T−20)` |
| FRP | `fsed_frp × ksed_frp/(ksed_frp + O2) × θ_frp^(T−20)` |

So a chamber flux equals the model's *actual* flux at the chamber's DO and temperature. It equals `fsed` only in anoxic water at 20 °C. To get `fsed` from a chamber flux, divide by the oxygen and temperature factor, which depends on the half-saturation constant `ksed`. `ksed` is itself a calibration parameter.


- **Oxygen sensitivity.** The previous calibration chose a very low `ksed_frp` (2.6), so P release is about 80× faster without oxygen than at 7 mg/L. Burger et al. found SRP release "largely independent of DO". In the 2026 data, dark and light chambers release similar PO4 even though DO fell during the deployment. Both point to a weaker oxygen dependence. With `ksed_frp` near 45 (the default), the chamber medians imply `fsed_frp` of 15–30 mg P/m²/d rather than 200–400.
- **Earlier starting values.** These were taken directly from the measured fluxes as `fsed`, with an oxygen correction for SOD only. If those measurements were made in oxygenated water, the NH4 and P starting values were too low by this factor.

## Comparison with the calibrated model

Passing the previous calibration's sediment parameters through the AED equations at the 2026 chamber conditions shows three things:

- **Deep zone (15–20 m):** NH4 and PO4 release are close to the chambers. They get there through very high anoxic rates that oxygen holds down.
- **10 m:** the calibration had effectively switched release off. The chambers show release comparable to the deeper sites.
- **SOD:** the model's sediment oxygen demand is several times the measured value at every depth. Almost all of the hypolimnetic oxygen depletion was being supplied by the sediment, with little left for water-column respiration.

The calibration fitted the in-lake data with sediment fluxes that are partly inconsistent with direct measurements. This is the motivation for using the chambers as a prior (@sec-calibration).

## Using the data in calibration {#sec-calibration}

### Where the data enter GLM-AED

GLM-AED's Constant2d sediment model sets the sediment–water fluxes of oxygen, ammonium and phosphate separately for each sediment zone. Each zone has three parameters per flux:

- a maximum rate (`fsed`);
- an oxygen half-saturation constant (`ksed`);
- a temperature coefficient (θ).

Each zone then reacts to the average water conditions over its depth band. The chambers sit at 20, 15 and 10 m. With the five-zone layout (@sec-zones), each site falls in a different zone, so each zone with data gets its own local measurement. Zones 4 and 5 (0–8 m) have no 2026 data and stay loosely constrained.

Constant2d rates do not change over time. A single parameter set cannot follow the drop in release between Burger et al. (2003–04) and 2026. The chamber data therefore describe the present-day (alum-era) sediment state. The Burger data describe the pre-alum state and suit a run that is scored on that period.

### The chambers as a prior

A chamber measures the actual flux at its own oxygen and temperature, not `fsed` (@sec-aed). Converting a measured flux to `fsed` depends on `ksed`, and `ksed` is itself uncertain. So a prior placed directly on `fsed` would carry a hidden assumption about the oxygen dependence. A better prior is one on the **flux the model would produce at the chamber's conditions**:

$$
\log F_\text{AED}(f_\text{sed}, k_\text{sed}, \theta \mid \text{DO}_\text{ch}, T_\text{ch}) \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\log \tilde F_\text{ch},\ \sigma^2\right),
\qquad \sigma^2 = \sigma_\text{rep}^2 + s^2/n
$$

- $\tilde F_\text{ch}$ is the chamber median for the zone and variable.
- $s^2/n$ is the sampling uncertainty of that median (spread between chambers, *n* chambers).
- $\sigma_\text{rep}$ (about 0.3 in log units, roughly ±35%) covers how well a few chambers represent a whole zone and season.

This is a joint prior on the three parameters. It ties them to the data only through the combination the chambers actually measured, at the oxygen and temperature they measured it. `fsed` and `ksed` can still trade off against each other: a high anoxic maximum with strong oxygen suppression, or a lower maximum with weak suppression. Which of these fits is left to the in-lake data. The hypolimnetic build-up of NH4 and DRP during stratification is what separates them. The chambers fix the flux under late-summer, partly oxygenated conditions; the hypolimnion record fixes how it changes as the bottom water goes anoxic.

### In practice

1. **Prior range.** Each zone with data gets limits set as multiples of the chamber-implied `fsed` (for example 0.3–3×), rather than the wide limits used before. Zones without data keep wide limits, or borrow a weaker prior from the nearest measured zone.
2. **Prior ensemble.** In an ensemble calibration (ES-MDA), the starting members are drawn around the chamber-implied values. The ensemble therefore starts from measured rates rather than from earlier calibrated rates.
3. **Prior term in the objective.** Each member's realized flux at the chamber conditions is computed from its parameters alone (no model run needed) and scored against the chamber median in log space. Because the term is a Gaussian log-likelihood in log space, adding it to the objective is equivalent to the Gaussian prior above. Its weight sets how strongly the prior holds against the lake data.

### What the prior adds

- **Splitting oxygen demand.** Bulk hypolimnetic oxygen depletion (AHOD) combines sediment and water-column demand. The lake profiles alone cannot separate them. The chamber SOD fixes the sediment share, so the rest has to come from water-column respiration of settling organic matter. This shifts calibration effort toward organic-matter supply and decay, rather than letting sediment demand absorb all of it.
- **Release at intermediate depths.** Without a prior, the calibration can switch release off in a zone (as it did at 10 m) and make up for it elsewhere. The chamber prior keeps a measured, non-zero release at each sampled depth.
- **Oxygen dependence.** The chambers and Burger et al. both point to weak oxygen control of P release. Together with the flux prior, a prior on `ksed_frp` centred well above the earlier calibrated value is consistent with both datasets.
- **A check on conflict with the lake data.** If the posterior moves far from the chamber prior, the zone fluxes and the in-lake data disagree. That points to another process, such as the organic-matter supply, the mixing or the P sinks, rather than to wrong chamber values.

## Caveats

- **Data status.** The 2026 chamber data are unpublished (Campbell, pers. comm., 2026). Keep the page to summaries until they are published, and check the published values when they appear.
- **Chamber DO.** Each nutrient flux is a regression over the whole deployment, while the DO in the chamber was falling. Using the lake DO as the chamber condition overstates the oxygen. The raw chamber time series (concentration and DO over time) would allow the oxygen dependence (`ksed`) to be fitted directly.
- **One deployment.** The 2026 data cover one late-summer deployment. Seasonal and year-to-year variation (a factor of about 5 between periods in Burger et al.) is not sampled. The 0.3 log tolerance is a judgement and could be widened.
- **No data for 0–3.5 m.** Zone 5 has no measurements, and zone 4 (3.5–8 m) has only Burger's 2003–04 data.
