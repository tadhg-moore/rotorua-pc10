Dadi et al. (2023)

Sudden eutrophication of an aluminum sulphate treated lake due to abrupt increase of internal phosphorus loading after three decades of mesotrophy. PDF

Click to expand: Summary

Key Findings - Lake Barleber (Germany), an artificial gravel pit lake successfully remediated with aluminium sulphate in 1986, remained mesotrophic for nearly 30 years before undergoing sudden re-eutrophication in 2016–2017, with TP increasing from ~40 µg L⁻¹ to ~270 µg L⁻¹ and massive cyanobacterial blooms developing. - Internal P loading from sediments was identified as the primary driver of re-eutrophication; estimated P release for 2017 was approximately 600 kg for the whole lake, consistent with sediment incubation experiments showing significant P release only under anoxic conditions. - The reducible (redox-dependent, Fe/Mn-bound) P fraction (BD-P) constituted 37–58% of total P in surface sediments, indicating high sensitivity to reductive release under anoxia. - Temperature and anoxia acted synergistically: anoxic high-temperature incubations produced SRP release rates approximately five times higher than anoxic low-temperature treatments (0.94 ± 0.23 vs ~0.17 mmol m⁻² d⁻¹), confirming temperature as a major amplifying factor. - Multiple P-retention indicators (NaOH-Al:NaOH-P, NaOH-Al:(NH₄Cl+BD)-P, and Sum-Al:Sum-Fe ratios) were all below recommended thresholds, demonstrating that the Al adsorption capacity of the sediment had been substantially exhausted after three decades. - Sediment focusing and low sedimentation rates (estimated at ~0.9 cm yr⁻¹) in this closed, autochthonous lake are proposed as factors that prolonged initial treatment effectiveness by keeping the Al-rich layer near the sediment surface.

Aluminium Sulphate Treatment: Results - The 1986 treatment applied 480 tonnes of aluminium sulphate (36 g Al m⁻², 5.7 mg Al L⁻¹ volumetric dosage), which successfully restored the lake to clear water within one year, with macrophytes re-establishing and TP remaining low (173–373 kg lake-wide) for nearly 30 years — well above the mean treatment longevity of ~11 years reported for Al-treated lakes globally. - P fractionation of the 40 cm sediment core identified a distinct NaOH-Al and NaOH-P peak at 28–34 cm depth, consistent with the depth expected for the 1986 Al application given the estimated sedimentation rate, confirming that the Al layer persisted in the sediment but had been progressively buried and diluted. - NaOH-Al:NaOH-P molar ratios across the sediment column (3–6) were uniformly below the recommended thresholds of 10 (Rydin and Welch 2000) and 12.6 (de Vicente et al. 2008), indicating that Al adsorption capacity had been exhausted relative to the P pool. - The Sum-Al:Sum-Fe ratio decreased approximately 5-fold from its value at the time of Al application, falling below the threshold of >3 proposed as necessary to prevent P release, and the NaOH-Al:(NH₄Cl+BD)-P ratio of ~3 in the upper sediment was far below the recommended threshold of >25. - High background sulphate concentrations (~500 mg L⁻¹ before treatment, rising after Al addition) promoted sulphide formation, which permanently immobilised Fe as iron sulphides and reduced the active iron pool competing with Al for P binding — a mechanism specific to aluminium sulphate treatment that would not apply to poly-aluminium chloride. - Al floc formed during treatment is susceptible to resuspension by wind and benthivorous fish activity (both documented at Lake Barleber), meaning the Al-rich layer was continuously mixed with new, lower-P-binding sediment rather than remaining as a discrete buried stratum, further eroding treatment effectiveness over time. - The tipping point towards re-eutrophication followed a three-stage mechanism: (1) gradual exhaustion of Al adsorption capacity causes non-bound P to accumulate in the BD-P pool; (2) the BD-P pool approaches saturation, making the system vulnerable to anoxic release; (3) wind mixing events rapidly transport released P from the hypolimnion to the photic zone, triggering bloom formation that then self-reinforces through elevated sediment oxygen demand and further SRP accumulation.

Implications & Recommendations - Al treatment of lakes is not a permanent solution; longevity depends on Al dose/area, hydraulic residence time, lake morphology (Osgood index), sedimentation rate, and background sulfate concentrations — the latter promoting sulphide formation that immobilises Fe and reduces the active iron pool available for P binding. - Re-eutrophication risk is likely to increase under climate warming, as longer and more intense stratification periods will extend anoxic conditions and raise hypolimnetic temperatures, accelerating organic matter mineralisation and internal P loading. - Regular post-treatment monitoring of sediment P fractionation and Al retention molar ratios in the upper 5 cm is essential to detect declining P adsorption capacity before re-eutrophication occurs. - Poly-aluminium chloride is recommended in preference to aluminium sulphate, given that the high background sulphate concentration in Lake Barleber contributed to sulphide formation and loss of the active iron P-binding pool. - Many treated lakes will require repeated aluminium application to sustain acceptable water quality; this should be planned as part of long-term lake management rather than treated as a one-off intervention.

2022

Hamill (2022)

Trend and State of Nutrients in Lake Rotorua Streams 2002–2022.
River Lake Ltd for Bay of Plenty Regional Council.
PDF

Click to expand: Summary

The report focuses on nine major streams contributing to Lake Rotorua’s water quality. - Nutrient trends were assessed using the seasonal Kendall test over three periods: 2002-2022, 2002-2010, and 2010-2019. - The analysis includes total nitrogen (TN), total phosphorus (TP), and dissolved reactive phosphorus (DRP) concentrations and loads.

Nutrient Concentrations and Stream Flows This section details the mean flow rates and nutrient concentrations in the streams entering Lake Rotorua.

  • The mean flow to Lake Rotorua from 2002-2022 was 11.8 m³/s, with Hamurana being the largest contributor at 2.63 m³/s.
  • The highest total nitrogen concentrations were found in Waiohewa (2839 mg/m³) and the highest total phosphorus concentrations in Waingaehe (123 mg/m³).
  • Streams like Hamurana, Awahou, and Waingaehe showed high concentrations of dissolved reactive phosphorus (DRP), with ratios of DRP to TP between 80-90%.

Trends in Nutrient Concentrations Over Time This section discusses the observed trends in nutrient concentrations across the monitored streams.

  • From 2002 to 2022, TN concentrations increased in Hamurana, Awahou, and Waiteti, particularly in Awahou and Waiteti since 2010.
  • TN concentrations decreased in Puarenga and possibly Ngongotaha, but this trend did not continue post-2010.
  • TP concentrations increased in Hamurana, Ngongotaha, Utuhina, Puarenga, Waingaehe, and Waiohewa, with trends continuing since 2010 in all but Waingaehe.

Nutrient Loads to Lake Rotorua

  • The nine main streams transported an average of 410 t/y of nitrogen and 28 t/y of phosphorus from 2002-2022.
  • The highest TN loads came from Awahou (71.1 t/y), Puarenga (67.4 t/y), and Hamurana (66.5 t/y).
  • TP loads were primarily contributed by Hamurana (7.5 t/y), Puarenga (5.1 t/y), and Awahou (3.9 t/y).

Mangeya (2022)

Groundwater Modelling Report.
Jacobs for Bay of Plenty Regional Council (Toi Moana).

Click to expand: Summary

Project Background and Objectives

The Bay of Plenty Regional Council engaged Jacobs to develop a groundwater flow model for the Rotorua Water Management Area to enhance water management practices. The Rotorua Water Management Area covers approximately 1,100 km². The model aims to improve understanding of groundwater systems and surface water interactions. It will inform groundwater allocation limits and assess various abstraction scenarios.

Scope of Work for Groundwater Modelling

The project involved several key tasks to develop and document the groundwater model.

  • Analyze available data and create a conceptual hydrogeological model.
  • Develop and calibrate a transient three-dimensional numerical groundwater flow model using MODFLOW.
  • Simulate predictive scenarios and document the model development and predictions.
  • Deliver the numerical model and associated files to the Bay of Plenty Regional Council.

Limitations of the Groundwater Modelling

  • The model does not account for certain factors that could affect groundwater and surface water quality.
  • Excludes the impact of groundwater extraction on water quality. Does not consider density effects from variable groundwater salinity or temperature. Ignores two-phase fluids in the active geothermal reservoir.

2021

McBride, MacCormick, and Verburg (2021)

Estimated catchment loads of nitrogen and phosphorus to the Rotorua Te Arawa Lakes.
ERI report 143, University of Waikato.
PDF

Click to expand: Summary

Importance and Context

  • Rotorua Te Arawa Lakes are culturally, historically, and ecologically significant.

  • Water quality varies with catchment characteristics: undeveloped (e.g., Tikitapu, Okataina) vs. highly developed agricultural/forestry (e.g., Rotorua, Rerewhakaaitu, Okaro).

  • Lake water quality is strongly influenced by nutrient inputs from surface and groundwater flows.

Methodology for Nutrient Loads

  • Used updated export coefficient approach and Overseer® modelling for nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P).

  • Land use areas calculated for surface and groundwater catchments.

  • Nutrient loss rates estimated per land use, accounting for local factors (e.g., pest impacts, forest removal).

  • Additional sources included geothermal inputs, septic systems, municipal wastewater, atmospheric deposition, and hydrological connections among lakes.

  • Attenuation estimated from physiography to produce a consistent regional nutrient budget.

Key Findings and Implications

  • Agricultural land contributes disproportionately to N and P loads.

  • Geothermal, atmospheric, and connected-lake sources are significant in some catchments.

  • Many catchments exceed ‘reference’ nutrient loads by 2–3×, complicating restoration.

  • Provides basis for assessing internal lake loading and guiding catchment/lake management strategies.

Example Nutrient Loads (kg/yr)

  • Lake Rotorua: N 721,427, P 56,760.5

2018

Me et al. (2018)

Modeling the effects of climate change on water quality of Lake Rotorua, New Zealand.

Click to expand: Summary

Modelling Hydrology and Water Quality This study integrates a catchment model with a lake water quality model to assess the impacts of nutrient load reductions and climate change on a eutrophic lake.

  • The study combines SWAT and DYRESM–CAEDYM models to simulate nutrient dynamics and water quality.
  • The models performed satisfactorily in simulating observed data, indicating accurate representation of key processes.
  • Nutrient load reductions from agricultural practices had minor effects on lake nutrient concentrations.
  • Climate change projections for 2090 showed significant impacts on nutrient levels and water quality.

Nutrient Load Reductions and Their Effects The study evaluates how reducing nutrient loads affects the trophic state of Lake Rotorua.

  • Simulations indicated that reducing fertilizer and wastewater irrigation had limited impact on lake nutrient concentrations.
  • The projected climate change for 2090 is expected to increase internal nutrient loading significantly.
  • Polymictic lakes like Lake Rotorua are particularly vulnerable to eutrophication due to climate change.

Climate Change Impacts on Water Quality This section discusses the influence of climate change on hydrological cycles and nutrient dynamics.

  • Increased precipitation is predicted to raise total phosphorus loads by 3.3%–16.5% in Danish streams.
  • A study in Slovenia projected total nitrogen losses could increase by 5.3% to 80.2% due to climate change.
  • Climate change is expected to directly affect lake water temperature and stratification, altering nutrient dynamics.

2016

Smith et al. (2016)

Phosphorus and nitrogen loading restraints are essential for successful eutrophication control of Lake Rotorua, New Zealand. PDF

Click to expand: Summary

Key Findings - Generalised additive models applied to a 14-year (2001–2015) dataset from Lake Rotorua found highly significant (p < 0.001) declines in total phosphorus (TP), total nitrogen (TN), and chlorophyll a (Chl-a) in surface waters, refuting claims that water quality had continued to decline despite mitigation. - Alum dosing in the Utuhina and Puarenga Stream inflows had a highly significant reducing effect on TP and Chl-a (p < 0.001), with near 4-fold reductions in Chl-a at maximum dosing rates, but no significant effect on TN. - Correlations of Chl-a with both TP and TN were highly significant, and the difference between the two correlation coefficients was not significant, indicating that algal productivity responds to both nutrients rather than either alone. - Bioassay studies in Lake Rotorua consistently demonstrated co-limitation of phytoplankton growth by both N and P, with spatial and temporal variation in which nutrient was more limiting. - Policies focused solely on reducing N loads risk stimulating dominance by heterocystous, N₂-fixing cyanobacteria (e.g. Dolichospermum, Aphanizomenon), whose bloom concentrations are strongly correlated with water column TP.

Implications & Recommendations - Both N and P loads to Lake Rotorua must be controlled simultaneously for effective eutrophication management; a single-nutrient strategy is insufficient and potentially counterproductive. - Alum dosing in tributary inflows is effective for short-term P reduction but is not recommended as a perpetual measure; its continuation should be contingent on progress in catchment-scale land-use change and nutrient runoff mitigation. - Achieving the target Trophic Level Index of 4.2 requires TN loads below 435 t y⁻¹ and TP loads below 37 t y⁻¹ — major reductions from current inputs dominated by agricultural land use (~75% of N load, ~45% of P load). - “Legacy P” stored in fertilised soils and streambed sediments, and long groundwater residence times (decades to over a century), mean that water quality responses to catchment management will be substantially lagged and require long-term commitment. - Continued monitoring of nutrients, Chl-a, and cyanobacterial biovolume should be maintained to track restoration trajectories and evaluate the relative contributions of alum dosing versus catchment management.

2015

Abell, McBride, and Hamilton (2015)

Lake Rotorua Treated Wastewater Discharge: Environmental Effects Study ERI Report 80, University of Waikato. PDF

Click to expand: Summary

Key Findings

  • The treated wastewater discharge contributes a measurable nutrient load to Lake Rotorua, which may influence the lake’s trophic status, algal growth, and water-quality trends.
  • There is evidence of spatial variability in how the discharge impacts the lake — e.g., nearer to the discharge site showing different water quality versus more remote parts of the lake.
  • The effectiveness of the current wastewater treatment and discharge regime is assessed and found to have limitations: even treated wastewater can contribute significant nutrient inputs if volumes are large and/or treatment is not optimised.
  • The report discusses the lake’s capacity to assimilate or dilute the nutrient load, and whether it is being exceeded or approaching thresholds that may degrade ecological health.

Implications & Recommendations

  • Management of wastewater inputs to Lake Rotorua must account not just for concentration of nutrients in the effluent but for volume of discharge and how that interacts with lake hydrodynamics (mixing, stratification, residence time).
  • Upgrading treatment processes, reducing nutrient concentrations further, or reducing the volume of discharge may be necessary to protect lake health.
  • Monitoring of water quality and ecological responses (algal blooms, oxygen levels, benthic habitat) should continue and be linked explicitly to discharge data to evaluate trends.
  • Lake management strategies should take a holistic view: catchment land-use, inflows, internal nutrient cycling, and wastewater discharges all interact to determine lake condition.

2012

Hamilton et al. (2012)

Predicting the effects of nutrient loads, management regimes and climate change on water quality of Lake Rotorua. (pers. comm. 2025-11-24)

Click to expand: Summary

Lake Rotorua’s Water Quality Challenges Lake Rotorua has faced significant water quality decline due to nutrient loads and land use changes, necessitating a comprehensive management strategy.

  • Lake area: 80 km², mean depth: 10 m.
  • Cultural significance to the Te Arawa people and vital for tourism and recreation.
  • Water quality deterioration began in the 1960s, linked to treated wastewater inputs and land use changes from forest to pasture.
  • Urban wastewater discharge ceased in 1991, but ongoing pastoral expansion, especially dairy farming, has continued to impact water quality.
  • Legacy effects from historical land use and nutrient pools in sediments complicate current management efforts.

Modelling Approach for Water Quality Management A modelling approach is essential for assessing the impacts of land use, climate change, and management actions on Lake Rotorua’s water quality.

  • Integrated multiple models: SimCLIM, ROTAN, CLUES, DYRESM-CAEDYM.
  • SimCLIM predicts climate data (temperature, rainfall) for the Rotorua catchment up to 2100.
  • ROTAN simulates streamflows and nitrate transport between surface water and groundwater.
  • CLUES estimates annual average phosphorus loads in streams.
  • DYRESM-CAEDYM models vertical distribution of water temperature and nutrient cycling relevant to trophic state.

Nutrient Load Reduction Requirements To improve water quality, significant reductions in nitrogen and phosphorus loads are necessary.

  • At least 350 tonnes of nitrogen and a similar amount of phosphorus must be removed annually.
  • Target Trophic Level Index (TLI) value set at 4.2 to restore water quality.
  • Climate change is expected to exacerbate challenges, increasing stratification and internal nutrient loading.
  • Internal nutrient release from sediments is comparable to external loads, complicating management.

Internal Nutrient Loading and Management Strategies Addressing internal nutrient loading is critical for achieving long-term water quality goals in Lake Rotorua.

  • Internal loading from sediments contributes significantly to nutrient levels, especially during stratification.
  • Chemical flocculants may reduce internal loading but have limited duration of effectiveness (approximately four years).
  • Effective sediment capping can offset water quality deterioration but requires prior reduction of catchment nutrient loads.

<details>
<summary>Click to expand: Summary</summary>

- Lake Barleber (Germany), an artificial gravel pit lake successfully remediated with aluminium sulphate in 1986, remained mesotrophic for nearly 30 years before undergoing sudden re-eutrophication in 2016–2017, with TP increasing from ~40 µg L⁻¹ to ~270 µg L⁻¹ and massive cyanobacterial blooms developing.

[PDF](https://atlas.boprc.govt.nz/api/v1/edms/document/A4440195/content)

<details>

<summary>Click to expand: Summary</summary>

The report focuses on nine major streams contributing to Lake Rotorua's water
quality. - Nutrient trends were assessed using the seasonal Kendall test over
three periods: 2002-2022, 2002-2010, and 2010-2019. - The analysis includes
total nitrogen (TN), total phosphorus (TP), and dissolved reactive phosphorus
(DRP) concentrations and loads.

*Nutrient Concentrations and Stream Flows* This section details the mean flow
rates and nutrient concentrations in the streams entering Lake Rotorua.

-   The mean flow to Lake Rotorua from 2002-2022 was 11.8 m³/s, with Hamurana
    being the largest contributor at 2.63 m³/s.
-   The highest total nitrogen concentrations were found in Waiohewa (2839
    mg/m³) and the highest total phosphorus concentrations in Waingaehe (123
    mg/m³).
-   Streams like Hamurana, Awahou, and Waingaehe showed high concentrations of
    dissolved reactive phosphorus (DRP), with ratios of DRP to TP between
    80-90%.

*Trends in Nutrient Concentrations Over Time* This section discusses the
observed trends in nutrient concentrations across the monitored streams.

-   From 2002 to 2022, TN concentrations increased in Hamurana, Awahou, and
    Waiteti, particularly in Awahou and Waiteti since 2010.
-   TN concentrations decreased in Puarenga and possibly Ngongotaha, but this
    trend did not continue post-2010.
-   TP concentrations increased in Hamurana, Ngongotaha, Utuhina, Puarenga,
    Waingaehe, and Waiohewa, with trends continuing since 2010 in all but
    Waingaehe.

*Nutrient Loads to Lake Rotorua*

-   The nine main streams transported an average of 410 t/y of nitrogen and 28
    t/y of phosphorus from 2002-2022.
-   The highest TN loads came from Awahou (71.1 t/y), Puarenga (67.4 t/y), and
    Hamurana (66.5 t/y).
-   TP loads were primarily contributed by Hamurana (7.5 t/y), Puarenga (5.1
    t/y), and Awahou (3.9 t/y).

### @mangeya2022

Groundwater Modelling Report.\
Jacobs for Bay of Plenty Regional Council (Toi Moana).

<details>

<summary>Click to expand: Summary</summary>

**Project Background and Objectives**

The Bay of Plenty Regional Council engaged Jacobs to develop a groundwater flow
model for the Rotorua Water Management Area to enhance water management
practices. The Rotorua Water Management Area covers approximately 1,100 km². The
model aims to improve understanding of groundwater systems and surface water
interactions. It will inform groundwater allocation limits and assess various
abstraction scenarios.

**Scope of Work for Groundwater Modelling**

The project involved several key tasks to develop and document the groundwater
model.

-   Analyze available data and create a conceptual hydrogeological model.
-   Develop and calibrate a transient three-dimensional numerical groundwater
    flow model using MODFLOW.
-   Simulate predictive scenarios and document the model development and
    predictions.
-   Deliver the numerical model and associated files to the Bay of Plenty
    Regional Council.

**Limitations of the Groundwater Modelling**

-   The model does not account for certain factors that could affect groundwater
    and surface water quality.
-   Excludes the impact of groundwater extraction on water quality. Does not
    consider density effects from variable groundwater salinity or temperature.
    Ignores two-phase fluids in the active geothermal reservoir.

</details>

## 2021

### @mcbride2021

Estimated catchment loads of nitrogen and phosphorus to the Rotorua Te Arawa
Lakes.\
ERI report 143, University of Waikato.\
[PDF](https://hdl.handle.net/10289/16725)

<details>

<summary>Click to expand: Summary</summary>

*Importance and Context*

-    Rotorua Te Arawa Lakes are culturally, historically, and ecologically
    significant.

-    Water quality varies with catchment characteristics: undeveloped (e.g.,
    Tikitapu, Okataina) vs. highly developed agricultural/forestry (e.g.,
    Rotorua, Rerewhakaaitu, Okaro).

-    Lake water quality is strongly influenced by nutrient inputs from surface
    and groundwater flows.

*Methodology for Nutrient Loads*

-   Used updated export coefficient approach and Overseer® modelling for
    nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P).

-    Land use areas calculated for surface and groundwater catchments.

-   Nutrient loss rates estimated per land use, accounting for local factors
    (e.g., pest impacts, forest removal).

-   Additional sources included geothermal inputs, septic systems, municipal
    wastewater, atmospheric deposition, and hydrological connections among
    lakes.

-   Attenuation estimated from physiography to produce a consistent regional
    nutrient budget.

*Key Findings and Implications*

-    Agricultural land contributes disproportionately to N and P loads.

-    Geothermal, atmospheric, and connected-lake sources are significant in some
    catchments.

-    Many catchments exceed ‘reference’ nutrient loads by 2–3×, complicating
    restoration.

-   Provides basis for assessing internal lake loading and guiding
    catchment/lake management strategies.

**Example Nutrient Loads (kg/yr)**

-   Lake Rotorua: N 721,427, P 56,760.5

</details>

## 2018

### @wang2018

Modeling the effects of climate change on water quality of Lake Rotorua, New
Zealand.

<details>

<summary>Click to expand: Summary</summary>

*Modelling Hydrology and Water Quality* This study integrates a catchment model
with a lake water quality model to assess the impacts of nutrient load
reductions and climate change on a eutrophic lake.

-   The study combines SWAT and DYRESM–CAEDYM models to simulate nutrient
    dynamics and water quality.
-   The models performed satisfactorily in simulating observed data, indicating
    accurate representation of key processes.
-   Nutrient load reductions from agricultural practices had minor effects on
    lake nutrient concentrations.
-   Climate change projections for 2090 showed significant impacts on nutrient
    levels and water quality.

*Nutrient Load Reductions and Their Effects* The study evaluates how reducing
nutrient loads affects the trophic state of Lake Rotorua.

-   Simulations indicated that reducing fertilizer and wastewater irrigation had
    limited impact on lake nutrient concentrations.
-   The projected climate change for 2090 is expected to increase internal
    nutrient loading significantly.
-   Polymictic lakes like Lake Rotorua are particularly vulnerable to
    eutrophication due to climate change.

*Climate Change Impacts on Water Quality* This section discusses the influence
of climate change on hydrological cycles and nutrient dynamics.

-   Increased precipitation is predicted to raise total phosphorus loads by
    3.3%–16.5% in Danish streams.
-   A study in Slovenia projected total nitrogen losses could increase by 5.3%
    to 80.2% due to climate change.
-   Climate change is expected to directly affect lake water temperature and
    stratification, altering nutrient dynamics.

## 2016

### @smith2016

Phosphorus and nitrogen loading restraints are essential for successful eutrophication control of Lake Rotorua, New Zealand. [PDF](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.5268/IW-6.2.998)

<details>
<summary>Click to expand: Summary</summary>

*Key Findings*
- Generalised additive models applied to a 14-year (2001–2015) dataset from Lake Rotorua found highly significant (p < 0.001) declines in total phosphorus (TP), total nitrogen (TN), and chlorophyll *a* (Chl-*a*) in surface waters, refuting claims that water quality had continued to decline despite mitigation.
- Alum dosing in the Utuhina and Puarenga Stream inflows had a highly significant reducing effect on TP and Chl-*a* (p < 0.001), with near 4-fold reductions in Chl-*a* at maximum dosing rates, but no significant effect on TN.
- Correlations of Chl-*a* with both TP and TN were highly significant, and the difference between the two correlation coefficients was not significant, indicating that algal productivity responds to both nutrients rather than either alone.
- Bioassay studies in Lake Rotorua consistently demonstrated co-limitation of phytoplankton growth by both N and P, with spatial and temporal variation in which nutrient was more limiting.
- Policies focused solely on reducing N loads risk stimulating dominance by heterocystous, N₂-fixing cyanobacteria (e.g. *Dolichospermum*, *Aphanizomenon*), whose bloom concentrations are strongly correlated with water column TP.

*Implications & Recommendations*
- Both N and P loads to Lake Rotorua must be controlled simultaneously for effective eutrophication management; a single-nutrient strategy is insufficient and potentially counterproductive.
- Alum dosing in tributary inflows is effective for short-term P reduction but is not recommended as a perpetual measure; its continuation should be contingent on progress in catchment-scale land-use change and nutrient runoff mitigation.
- Achieving the target Trophic Level Index of 4.2 requires TN loads below 435 t y⁻¹ and TP loads below 37 t y⁻¹ — major reductions from current inputs dominated by agricultural land use (~75% of N load, ~45% of P load).
- "Legacy P" stored in fertilised soils and streambed sediments, and long groundwater residence times (decades to over a century), mean that water quality responses to catchment management will be substantially lagged and require long-term commitment.
- Continued monitoring of nutrients, Chl-*a*, and cyanobacterial biovolume should be maintained to track restoration trajectories and evaluate the relative contributions of alum dosing versus catchment management.

</details>

## 2015

### @abell2015

Lake Rotorua Treated Wastewater Discharge: Environmental Effects Study ERI
Report 80, University of Waikato. [PDF](https://hdl.handle.net/10289/12474)

<details>

<summary>Click to expand: Summary</summary>

*Key Findings*

-   The treated wastewater discharge contributes a measurable nutrient load to
    Lake Rotorua, which may influence the lake’s trophic status, algal growth,
    and water-quality trends.
-   There is evidence of spatial variability in how the discharge impacts the
    lake — e.g., nearer to the discharge site showing different water quality
    versus more remote parts of the lake.
-   The effectiveness of the current wastewater treatment and discharge regime
    is assessed and found to have limitations: even treated wastewater can
    contribute significant nutrient inputs if volumes are large and/or treatment
    is not optimised.
-   The report discusses the lake’s capacity to assimilate or dilute the
    nutrient load, and whether it is being exceeded or approaching thresholds
    that may degrade ecological health.

*Implications & Recommendations*

-   Management of wastewater inputs to Lake Rotorua must account not just for
    concentration of nutrients in the effluent but for volume of discharge and
    how that interacts with lake hydrodynamics (mixing, stratification,
    residence time).
-   Upgrading treatment processes, reducing nutrient concentrations further, or
    reducing the volume of discharge may be necessary to protect lake health.
-   Monitoring of water quality and ecological responses (algal blooms, oxygen
    levels, benthic habitat) should continue and be linked explicitly to
    discharge data to evaluate trends.
-   Lake management strategies should take a holistic view: catchment land-use,
    inflows, internal nutrient cycling, and wastewater discharges all interact
    to determine lake condition.

</details>

## 2012

### @hamilton2012

Predicting the effects of nutrient loads, management regimes and climate change
on water quality of Lake Rotorua. (pers. comm. 2025-11-24)

<details>

<summary>Click to expand: Summary</summary>

*Lake Rotorua's Water Quality Challenges* Lake Rotorua has faced significant
water quality decline due to nutrient loads and land use changes, necessitating
a comprehensive management strategy.

-   Lake area: 80 km², mean depth: 10 m.
-   Cultural significance to the Te Arawa people and vital for tourism and
    recreation.
-   Water quality deterioration began in the 1960s, linked to treated wastewater
    inputs and land use changes from forest to pasture.
-   Urban wastewater discharge ceased in 1991, but ongoing pastoral expansion,
    especially dairy farming, has continued to impact water quality.
-   Legacy effects from historical land use and nutrient pools in sediments
    complicate current management efforts.

*Modelling Approach for Water Quality Management* A modelling approach is
essential for assessing the impacts of land use, climate change, and management
actions on Lake Rotorua's water quality.

-   Integrated multiple models: SimCLIM, ROTAN, CLUES, DYRESM-CAEDYM.
-   SimCLIM predicts climate data (temperature, rainfall) for the Rotorua
    catchment up to 2100.
-   ROTAN simulates streamflows and nitrate transport between surface water and
    groundwater.
-   CLUES estimates annual average phosphorus loads in streams.
-   DYRESM-CAEDYM models vertical distribution of water temperature and nutrient
    cycling relevant to trophic state.

*Nutrient Load Reduction Requirements* To improve water quality, significant
reductions in nitrogen and phosphorus loads are necessary.

-   At least 350 tonnes of nitrogen and a similar amount of phosphorus must be
    removed annually.
-   Target Trophic Level Index (TLI) value set at 4.2 to restore water quality.
-   Climate change is expected to exacerbate challenges, increasing
    stratification and internal nutrient loading.
-   Internal nutrient release from sediments is comparable to external loads,
    complicating management.

*Internal Nutrient Loading and Management Strategies* Addressing internal
nutrient loading is critical for achieving long-term water quality goals in Lake
Rotorua.

-   Internal loading from sediments contributes significantly to nutrient
    levels, especially during stratification.
-   Chemical flocculants may reduce internal loading but have limited duration
    of effectiveness (approximately four years).
-   Effective sediment capping can offset water quality deterioration but
    requires prior reduction of catchment nutrient loads.
