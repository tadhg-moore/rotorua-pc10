ENSO and Lake Rotorua

Historical influence on meteorology, inflows and the lake, and how ENSO is represented in the downscaled CMIP6 data

Note

Status: work in progress. Sections 1-5 are complete; a synthesis section remains to be finalised. Analysis scripts are in calibration/enso/ (numbered 00-07), with working notes NOTE_enso_met.md and NOTE_enso_lake.md.

1. Approach and data

ENSO index. Following the Stats NZ ENSO indicator, ENSO phase is the 3-month mean Southern Oscillation Index (SOI): at or above +1 is La Niña, at or below -1 is El Niño. The Stats NZ / Earth Sciences NZ series (1990-2025) is used directly; before 1990 the NOAA CPC SOI is rescaled to it (r = 0.97 over the overlap). ONI (Niño3.4) is a sensitivity check; the IPO tripole index is used as a confounder. 2. Meteorology: the ENSO signal at Rotorua

La Niña tends to bring more easterly and north-easterly flow, and wetter, warmer conditions; El Niño more westerly flow, as the Stats NZ page describes. The strongest signal is wind direction, not wind speed.

Key results (1991-2025, seasonal means):

  • SOI explains a modest share of rain (r about 0.2-0.4 by season) and winter-spring temperature.
  • After controlling for the IPO and a linear trend, the temperature signal disappears and the rain signal weakens: much of the apparent SOI-temperature link is shared warming trend and IPO phase.
  • Wind: La Niña shifts flow towards the east/north-east (west-east component r from -0.19 to -0.42 at station 1770; MetService hourly direction agrees), the clearest ENSO fingerprint in the record.
  • ONI gives the same pattern with the opposite sign (SOI and ONI correlate at -0.83), so the conclusions do not depend on the index.

3. Inflows and the lake

3.1 Inflows

On the 50-year Ngongotaha record (1975-2025) La Niña seasons are wetter:

Mean flow is about 5-12% higher per +1 SOI (significant for summer and spring only), maxima respond more (+17 to +28%), and El Niño seasons run 8-19% below average. With IPO and trend controlled the direction is retained but significance is lost. Of 24 site x metric x season tests only the Ngongotaha summer and spring means reach p < 0.05, so this is a consistent but modest tendency. Measured records for Puarenga, Waingaehe and Kuirau are now in the pipeline (measured_inflow_daily target).

4. AEME counterfactual hindcast

The calibrated AEME model (alum refit member H1b_B143, alum on, 2005-2022) was run with the actual meteorology and then four times with the meteorology of every El Niño/La Niña year replaced by a randomly drawn neutral year (hourly, same calendar day). Inflows, alum dosing and parameters were unchanged, so the difference isolates the meteorological ENSO effect. Years are classed by the October-March mean SOI (at or below -0.75 El Niño, at or above +0.75 La Niña): El Niño 2010 and 2016, La Niña 2008, 2009, 2011, 2012, 2021 and 2022.

The meteorological ENSO effect on temperature, stratification, chlorophyll and phosphorus is within the year-to-year spread of neutral weather. La Niña years show somewhat more low oxygen (about 13 more days below 4 mg/L, 8 more anoxic days per year), but this comes almost entirely from the year to June 2022, when a warm November-January (1.5-2 °C above the neutral draws) pushed the modelled bottom water to about 1 mg/L; the other La Niña years show little. This is one event at a model threshold, with only 2 El Niño and 6 La Niña years, one parameter member and met-only changes (the wetter La Niña inflows are not included). Details and caveats, including a warming-trend bias in the neutral donor years, are in calibration/enso/NOTE_enso_hindcast.md.

5. ENSO in the downscaled CMIP6 data

The downscaled CMIP6 data (7 GCMs, one realisation each) are free-running, hold only local variables (tas, pr, rsds, hurs, sfcWind) and no Niño3.4 or SOI, so ENSO cannot be tracked event by event. It is assessed statistically from its imprint on the local record, against ERA5 1988-2024 (GCM window 1978-2014, also 37 years). Bias-corrected versions exist only for pr and tas.

Variability. Interannual variability is realistic: the coefficient of variation of seasonal anomalies is 0.29-0.38 for rain (ERA5 0.30), 0.042-0.056 for temperature (0.046) and 0.074-0.093 for wind speed (0.065). The share of variance in the 2-7 year ENSO band (0.04-0.19) is close to ERA5 (0.07-0.16), but it is also close to what red noise gives, in observations as well as the models, so this test cannot confirm or refute ENSO.

Future. The interannual standard deviation of seasonal anomalies in 2050-2099 relative to 1965-2014 has a median ratio of 0.93-1.04 for every variable and SSP, with GCM ranges that include 1 (0.8-1.35), and the ENSO-band share changes by less than 0.03. There is no robust change in local interannual variability.

Conclusion. The downscaled CMIP6 data reproduce the size and main co-variability of Rotorua’s interannual weather, but they cannot show that ENSO itself is captured, and spring co-variability is not reproduced by several GCMs. They should not be used for year-by-year or ENSO-phase-conditional statements. Scoring each GCM properly would need parent-GCM Niño3.4 and pressure (out of scope here). Details are in calibration/enso/NOTE_enso_cmip6.md.

6. Summary so far

  1. ENSO has a measurable but modest imprint on Rotorua: the clearest signals are wind direction, rainfall and inflow, with La Niña wetter and more easterly.
  2. The temperature response is not separable from the warming trend and IPO phase with the available record.
  3. No detectable ENSO signal in lake nutrients, chlorophyll, Secchi or bottom oxygen in the observations; the record is short and noisy and alum and load changes dominate.
  4. The AEME hindcast shows the meteorological ENSO effect on stratification, chlorophyll and phosphorus is within normal weather variability; the only notable effect is more low oxygen in the La Niña year to June 2022.
  5. The downscaled CMIP6 data match Rotorua’s interannual variability but cannot show that ENSO is captured (and spring co-variability is not reproduced by several GCMs), so they do not support ENSO-conditioned future statements. Future ENSO change is unquantified.

4. The AEME hindcast shows the meteorological ENSO effect on stratification, chlorophyll and phosphorus is within normal weather variability; the only notable effect is more low oxygen in the La Niña year to June 2022.
5. The downscaled CMIP6 data match Rotorua's interannual variability but cannot show that ENSO is captured (and spring co-variability is not reproduced by several GCMs), so they do not support ENSO-conditioned future statements. Future ENSO change is unquantified.
